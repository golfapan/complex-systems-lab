Complex vector spaces, bases, and phase

Why do two states with the same obvious probabilities behave differently?

The idea

Quantum superposition is vector addition with complex coefficients. A basis gives coordinates for a state, and an inner product compares states through amplitudes. Its complex conjugation is essential: it produces a nonnegative norm and makes orthogonality a meaningful statement about distinguishable directions in state space.

A global phase multiplies every component equally and leaves measurement probabilities unchanged. Relative phase changes one component compared with another and can change interference. Therefore replacing amplitudes by a list of squared magnitudes loses information. The missing phase may become visible after a change of measurement basis.

Read the relationship

|ψ⟩=α|0⟩+β|1⟩; |α|²+|β|²=1; ⟨φ|ψ⟩=Σφⱼ*ψⱼ

The star means complex conjugation. A basis change changes coordinates, not the underlying physical preparation.

Work through an example

  1. Define |+⟩=(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩=(|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2.
  2. Both give probabilities one half and one half when measured in the zero/one basis.
  3. Their inner product is (1−1)/2=0, so another measurement can distinguish them perfectly.

When it applies

A state vector is not an ordinary probability vector. The basis and phase convention must remain consistent when calculating amplitudes.

Check your understanding

Would multiplying both α and β by the same eⁱφ change a measurement prediction?

No. That common global phase cancels from all Born probabilities; changing only their relative phase can matter.

Why ODE Solutions Exist—and When They Are Unique

Can the same equation and initial value allow more than one future?

The idea

Solving an initial-value problem first requires knowing that a solution exists and that the data identify it uniquely. A common local theorem assumes continuity in time and local Lipschitz dependence on state. Continuity alone is generally too weak to exclude multiple solutions.

The integral form of an ODE also suggests a construction: repeatedly substitute a guessed function into the right-hand side. Under suitable conditions, Picard iteration converges to the unique solution on a short interval. Local existence, extension to all future times, continuous dependence and long-term stability are separate issues.

Read the relationship

x(t)=x₀+∫ₜ₀ᵗf(s,x(s))ds x′=x², x(0)=a>0 ⇒ x=a/(1−at)

A local Lipschitz bound controls how much f changes when its state input changes. The displayed solution blows up at t=1/a.

Work through an example

  1. Consider x′=√|x| with x(0)=0.
  2. One solution stays at zero forever.
  3. For any waiting time a≥0, another stays zero until a and then follows x=(t−a)²/4; the right-hand side is not locally Lipschitz at zero.

When it applies

Local uniqueness does not prevent finite-time blow-up. Initial-value theory and two-endpoint boundary-value theory ask different questions and have different guarantees.

Check your understanding

Does continuous dependence imply nearby solutions eventually come closer?

No. It bounds how perturbations affect solutions on a specified finite interval. An exponentially growing system can still satisfy continuous dependence.

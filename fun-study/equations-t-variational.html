Variation, Constraints and Equations of Motion

Can an equation of motion come from a principle about whole paths?

The idea

Variational calculus changes an entire candidate path, rather than just a few parameters. An action assigns a number to each path. Requiring its first variation to vanish produces the Euler–Lagrange equation under suitable endpoint and smoothness assumptions.

Stationary does not necessarily mean minimal. Constraints can be handled through independent coordinates or multipliers, depending on their type. Similar language appears in control and weak PDE formulations, but their functionals, admissible paths and boundary terms must be specified separately.

Read the relationship

S[q]=∫L(q,q̇,t)dt; δS=0; d/dt(∂L/∂q̇)−∂L/∂q=0

L is the Lagrangian and S the action. δ denotes a perturbation of the path; fixed endpoint positions remove the relevant integration-by-parts boundary term.

Work through an example

  1. For a spring, choose L=mq̇²/2−kq²/2: kinetic energy minus potential energy.
  2. The derivatives are ∂L/∂q̇=mq̇ and ∂L/∂q=−kq.
  3. Euler–Lagrange gives mq̈+kq=0. A damping force requires an additional force framework; it is not the same conservative potential.

When it applies

Nonsmooth paths and nonholonomic constraints need care. Deriving a stationary equation does not establish existence, minimality or stability of every solution.

Check your understanding

Does δS=0 prove that the path minimises action?

No. A stationary point may be a minimum, maximum or saddle; further variation analysis is required.

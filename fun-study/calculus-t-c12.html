First-Order Differential Equations

How can a rule for change determine a whole unknown function?

The idea

A differential equation relates an unknown function to its derivatives. A direction field can suggest how solutions move before we have a formula. Choosing a solution method starts with structure: separable equations split time and state factors; linear equations can combine into a product derivative.

An initial value is part of the problem, not an optional finishing touch. It selects a curve when existence and uniqueness conditions allow. Solutions need not be elementary formulas: an integral representation, implicit relation or controlled numerical approximation can still answer the original question.

Read the relationship

y′+p(t)y=q(t), μ=e^(∫p dt) (μy)′=μq For y′=a(t)b(y): ∫dy/b(y)=∫a(t)dt+C

μ is an integrating factor chosen so that μ′=pμ. Before dividing by b(y), preserve any equilibrium solutions with b(y)=0.

Work through an example

  1. Solve y′+2y=0 with y(0)=3.
  2. Multiplying by e²ᵗ gives (e²ᵗy)′=0, so e²ᵗy=C.
  3. The initial value fixes C=3, giving y=3e⁻²ᵗ; substitution checks both equation and initial value.

When it applies

A continuous right-hand side does not by itself guarantee a unique solution. Every manipulation and solution formula must stay within its valid interval.

Check your understanding

For y′=y(1−y), which solutions might division by y(1−y) lose?

The equilibria y=0 and y=1. Check them before dividing, even if a general nonconstant solution is also found.

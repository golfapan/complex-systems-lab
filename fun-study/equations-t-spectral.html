Operators, Spectra and Function Spaces

How do matrix eigenvectors become spatial modes?

The idea

An operator turns one function into another. Differentiation, integration and translation are examples. An eigenfunction changes only by a scalar factor under the operator, allowing a field evolution to be decomposed into modes with simpler individual behaviour.

Unlike a finite matrix, a differential operator needs a domain and boundary conditions as well as a formula. The same derivative expression can have different spectra on different function spaces. This makes boundary assumptions part of the mathematics, not merely a plotting choice.

Read the relationship

A=−d²/dx² on 0<x<π with zero endpoint values; A sin(nx)=n²sin(nx)

sin(nx) is an eigenfunction and n² its eigenvalue. For heat evolution uₜ=−κAu, this mode decays as e^(−κn²t).

Work through an example

  1. Begin with u(x,0)=sin x+0.1sin(10x), taking κ=1.
  2. At t=0.1, the first amplitude is e⁻⁰·¹≈0.9048.
  3. The high-frequency amplitude is 0.1e⁻¹⁰≈0.00000454, explaining smoothing and difficult backward recovery.

When it applies

Non-self-adjoint operators may have nonorthogonal modes and transient growth; continuous spectra need not be discrete sums. Truncation errors still require checking.

Check your understanding

Can the same sine basis automatically be retained after switching to periodic boundaries?

No. The admissible function space changes, and the corresponding periodic modes must be chosen.

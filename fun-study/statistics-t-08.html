Large numbers, the central limit theorem, and Monte Carlo

Why does averaging often make a noisy estimate more reliable?

The idea

The law of large numbers explains why a sample average can approach its expectation as observations accumulate. The central limit theorem describes the shape and scale of its remaining fluctuations under suitable assumptions. These are different statements: convergence of an average is not the same as a good normal approximation at a particular sample size.

Monte Carlo uses random samples to approximate an expectation or integral. For independent observations with finite variance, the standard error of their mean falls like 1/√n. This is a slow improvement: halving the error usually requires four times as many samples. Correlation can substantially reduce the effective information gained.

Read the relationship

SE(X̄)=σ/√n; (X̄−μ)/(σ/√n) approaches N(0,1) under the usual i.i.d. finite-variance CLT

σ is the population standard deviation; standard error measures uncertainty in an estimator, not the spread of individual observations.

Work through an example

  1. Consider independent clicks with true probability p=0.25. Each click has variance p(1−p)=0.1875.
  2. For 100 clicks, the sample proportion's standard error is √(0.1875/100)≈0.0433.
  3. For 400 clicks it is about 0.02165: four times the data gives half the standard error.

When it applies

There is no universal rule that n≥30 makes every distribution normal enough. Heavy tails, dependence, and rare events require care.

Check your understanding

Does a sample mean getting steadier mean the underlying individual outcomes become less variable?

No. Averaging improves the estimate; it does not reduce the variance of each original observation.

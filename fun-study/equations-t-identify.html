System Identification and Equation Discovery

Are you estimating a coefficient, a formula or an entire state model?

The idea

System identification begins by choosing the kind of unknown. You might fit a few parameters in known physics, select terms from a candidate library, learn a black-box vector field or infer hidden-state dynamics. These choices need different data and support different interpretations.

Equation discovery can regress estimated derivatives against candidate functions. Data coverage matters as much as the fitting technique. A system observed only near one equilibrium may provide no way to distinguish mechanisms that behave differently elsewhere; multiple initial conditions and informative inputs can help.

Read the relationship

Ẋ≈Θ(X)Ξ; candidate example: Θ(x)=[1,x,x²]

Θ is a library of candidate functions and Ξ their coefficients. Derivatives can be estimated from measurements or avoided through suitable integral or weak formulations.

Work through an example

  1. Suppose the real rule is ẋ=−2x. Rich noise-free data support coefficients (0,−2,0).
  2. If every observation instead has x=0 and ẋ=0, arbitrary linear and quadratic coefficients fit those measurements.
  3. Exciting the system away from zero supplies information the original dataset lacked.

When it applies

Differentiation amplifies noise, and a missing library term can force a misleading combination of remaining terms. Sparse does not automatically mean causal or physically true.

Check your understanding

Can an accurate polynomial fit be trusted ten times beyond its training state range?

Not without testing. Previously unexcited terms may dominate in that region.

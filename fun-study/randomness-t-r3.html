Randomness and Regularity / 03

Chaos and the horizon of useful prediction

Why can better initial measurements buy only a little extra forecast time?

The idea

Chaotic dynamics can amplify tiny initial differences while remaining deterministic. During an appropriate local growth stage, errors often grow roughly exponentially. Finite initial precision then sets a horizon beyond which a requested trajectory accuracy is no longer justified. Improving precision helps, but the gain in time is logarithmic.

Sensitive dependence is one part of diagnosing chaos, not a complete test by itself. A simple unbounded unstable system also separates trajectories exponentially. Reliable analysis considers bounded behavior, recurrent structure, nonperiodicity, and suitable Lyapunov calculations. Long-term distributions can remain informative even when individual trajectories fail to stay aligned.

Read the relationship

δn≈δ₀e^(λn); T≈log(tolerance/initial error)/λ for λ>0

δ is a small perturbation and λ is an effective growth rate over the stage being analyzed; n may count map iterations.

Work through an example

  1. Suppose λ≈log 2 per step, initial error is 10⁻⁶, and acceptable error is 10⁻².
  2. The estimated horizon is log(10⁴)/log 2≈13.3 steps.
  3. Ten times better initial precision adds about log 10/log 2≈3.3 steps, not ten times the horizon.

When it applies

Finite-distance trajectories eventually saturate on bounded sets. Their saturated separation is not a valid substitute for tangent-space exponent estimation.

Check your understanding

Does a positive short-time separation rate prove chaos?

No. It may describe transient growth or ordinary instability; additional dynamical evidence is needed.

Randomness and Regularity / 04

From random walks to diffusion

How does a collection of coin-toss steps turn into a smooth density equation?

The idea

In a symmetric random walk, each step moves left or right by a fixed distance. The next position distribution is the average of two shifted distributions. A diffusion limit keeps the ratio of squared step length to time step fixed while taking finer spatial and temporal scales. Taylor expansion then connects that discrete averaging rule to a second spatial derivative.

The resulting heat equation predicts a spreading density. It is not a statement that an individual path becomes smooth. Finite-step walks retain lattice and parity restrictions, whereas a Gaussian density is a continuous approximation. Distinguishing the exact discrete distribution from its limit helps explain both simulations and their errors.

Read the relationship

pt=Dpxx; D=a²/(2Δt); Var(Xt)=2Dt for a walk starting at zero

Here a is step length and Δt is time per step. D has units of length squared per time.

Work through an example

  1. Take a=1 and Δt=1, so D=1/2.
  2. After 100 independent steps, the endpoint variance is 100 and its standard deviation is ten.
  3. An endpoint need not be ten units away: ten describes distribution width. The exact endpoint distribution is binomial on the permitted lattice sites.

When it applies

A diffusion approximation requires a suitable scaling limit and assumptions about steps. Bias, dependence, or heavy-tailed jumps can lead to other limits.

Check your understanding

Does doubling the number of simulated walkers double the process time?

No. It improves sampling of the same-time distribution; adding steps changes the elapsed process time.

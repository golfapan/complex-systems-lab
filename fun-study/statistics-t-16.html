Bias, variance, regularization, and MAP

Why can a deliberately less flexible model predict better?

The idea

Prediction error can come from irreducible outcome noise, systematic approximation error, and sensitivity to the particular training sample. A flexible model may fit training data closely yet vary greatly across new training samples. Regularization trades some fit for a preference such as smaller coefficients or smoother functions.

A penalty is a modeling choice, not a universal cure. Ridge penalizes squared coefficients; a Gaussian parameter prior can connect it to a maximum a posteriori estimate under compatible likelihood and scaling assumptions. MAP selects one parameter value, whereas full Bayesian prediction averages over a posterior distribution.

Read the relationship

J(β)=Σ(yᵢ−xᵢᵀβ)²+λΣβⱼ²; expected squared error=noise+bias²+variance

λ controls penalty strength. The familiar bias–variance decomposition uses a specified source of randomness and squared prediction loss.

Work through an example

  1. Minimize J(b)=(b−3)²+2b².
  2. Expanding gives 3b²−6b+9; the derivative 6b−6 vanishes at b=1.
  3. Without the penalty, the optimum is three. Shrinkage worsens this training fit but may reduce sensitivity to noisy samples.

When it applies

Regularization cannot fix leaked information, wrong labels, or an inappropriate target population. Its strength should be selected without reusing final test data.

Check your understanding

Does shrinking every coefficient prove a model will generalize better?

No. The useful tradeoff depends on data, features, loss, and task; excessive shrinkage can underfit.

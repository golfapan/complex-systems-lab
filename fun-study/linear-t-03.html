Elimination and LU: Solving Without Building an Inverse

How can the work of solving one linear system be reused?

The idea

Elimination replaces equations by equivalent combinations until the unknowns can be recovered by substitution. A pivot identifies an independent constraint; a zero row can reveal redundancy or an inconsistency. Counting equations alone therefore does not tell us whether a solution exists or is unique.

An LU factorisation stores the elimination process. After a suitable row permutation, A becomes a lower-triangular factor times an upper-triangular factor. For many different right-hand sides, the factors can be reused, reducing each new solve to two triangular systems. An inverse is useful theoretically but is rarely the best computational starting point.

Read the relationship

PA=LU Ly=Pb, then Ux=y Square A is invertible exactly when rank(A)=n

P permutes rows, L stores elimination multipliers and U is upper triangular. Pivoting helps avoid numerically troublesome divisions.

Work through an example

  1. Solve 2x+y=5 and 4x+3y=11.
  2. Subtract twice the first equation from the second to obtain y=1, then back-substitute to get x=2.
  3. Here L=[[1,0],[2,1]] and U=[[2,1],[0,1]], retaining the same elimination structure.

When it applies

An invertible matrix may still be ill-conditioned. Tiny pivots and finite-precision data require care; a unique exact solution is not automatically a reliable numerical answer.

Check your understanding

Why not compute A⁻¹ every time the right-hand side changes?

A suitable factorisation can be computed once and reused for efficient, stable solves without explicitly constructing the inverse.

Numerical Results You Can Trust

How can a stable equation produce a growing numerical answer?

The idea

A numerical answer carries several kinds of uncertainty. Conditioning describes sensitivity already present in the mathematical problem; truncation error comes from approximation; rounding comes from finite arithmetic. Algorithmic stability concerns whether the computation amplifies these errors in harmful ways.

Smaller steps often reduce truncation error, but accuracy is not the same as stability. Local error assumes an exact starting point for one step; global error also carries previous errors through many steps. A convergence order describes an asymptotic regime, not a promise that every chosen step is already small enough.

Read the relationship

Euler: yn+1=yn+h f(tn,yn) y′=−ky ⇒ yn+1=(1−kh)yn Asymptotic decay for this scheme: 0<kh<2

h is the time step. The factor 1−kh is the numerical amplification factor; compare its magnitude with 1.

Work through an example

  1. The exact solution of y′=−2y decays exponentially.
  2. Choose explicit Euler with h=1.2: the step factor is 1−2.4=−1.4.
  3. Computed values alternate sign and grow. This is a discretisation failure, not a new physical instability of the original equation.

When it applies

Stable output can still be inaccurate. Stiff systems may force tiny explicit steps even when the visible solution is smooth; use appropriate methods and independent convergence checks.

Check your understanding

How should h and h/2 results be compared?

At the same physical times, using the quantities of interest. Only use order-based error estimates after checking that the calculation is in the expected convergence regime.

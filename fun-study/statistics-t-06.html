Expectation, variance, and prediction loss

Can the best numerical prediction be a value that never occurs?

The idea

Expectation is a probability-weighted average over possible outcomes. It summarizes the distribution rather than describing what must happen on one trial. Variance measures squared spread around that mean. Its units are squared, while standard deviation returns to the units of the original variable.

A prediction becomes optimal only relative to a stated loss. Squared error favors the conditional mean; absolute error favors a median. The decomposition of expected squared error explains why moving away from the mean adds a nonnegative penalty. A decision problem may instead require asymmetric costs or a full probability distribution.

Read the relationship

E[(X−a)²]=Var(X)+(a−μ)²; Var(X)=E[X²]−μ²

μ=E[X], and a is a constant prediction. The identity requires a finite second moment.

Work through an example

  1. A reward is zero with probability 0.8 and ten with probability 0.2.
  2. Its mean is 2 and its second moment is 20, so its variance is 20−2²=16.
  3. The best constant under squared loss is 2, even though a single reward is never 2.

When it applies

In general E[g(X)] is not g(E[X]). For dependent variables, the variance of a sum includes covariance terms.

Check your understanding

Would predicting ten be optimal merely because it is the largest possible reward?

No. Prediction accuracy depends on the loss and probabilities, not on which possible outcome is most attractive.

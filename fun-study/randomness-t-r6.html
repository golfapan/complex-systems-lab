Randomness and Regularity / 06

Why averages can become stable

What makes a large collection more reliable than one noisy observation?

The idea

Averaging suppresses fluctuations when different contributions supply sufficiently independent information. For independent identically distributed variables, the mean's variance is the individual variance divided by sample count. The law of large numbers and central limit theorem explain complementary parts of this behavior under their respective assumptions.

Dependence is the key limitation. Shared disturbances can make many measurements rise and fall together, so increasing their number may add much less information than expected. Near collective transitions, long-range correlations can also undermine naive averaging. The covariance terms tell us exactly what an independence argument leaves out.

Read the relationship

Var(X̄)=N⁻²[ΣVar(Xᵢ)+2Σi<j Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ)]; i.i.d. case: σ²/N

N is the number averaged. The variance formula applies whenever the relevant second moments exist.

Work through an example

  1. A 100-step symmetric walk has endpoint variance 100.
  2. The mean endpoint of 100 independent walkers has standard deviation √(100/100)=1.
  3. For 10,000 walkers the mean's standard deviation is 0.1, although each individual walk still has standard deviation ten.

When it applies

Large N alone is insufficient if observations are strongly correlated or their variance does not exist. Always identify the actual independent units.

Check your understanding

Does averaging make each underlying event less random?

No. It can stabilize an aggregate estimate without changing the distribution of individual events.

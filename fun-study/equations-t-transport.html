Probability Transport and Optimal Transport

How do moving samples describe a changing distribution?

The idea

The particle viewpoint follows positions under a velocity field. The density viewpoint tracks how their probability mass is carried and compressed. These are two descriptions of related dynamics: samples may move while the density changes because nearby trajectories converge or spread apart.

Optimal transport asks an additional question: which feasible movement has the lowest specified cost? Choosing a smooth interpolation between distributions does not by itself solve that optimisation. Endpoint pairing, the cost and the admissible paths are essential parts of the claim.

Read the relationship

Ẋ=v(X,t); ∂ₜp+∇·(pv)=0; d/dt log p(X(t),t)=−∇·v

p is a density, not a particle position. The logarithmic identity requires suitable smoothness and positive density along the trajectory.

Work through an example

  1. Use v(x)=−x, so X(t)=e⁻ᵗX(0).
  2. Starting from a standard Gaussian, the variance becomes e⁻²ᵗ and the central density rises.
  3. Since v′=−1, log density along each trajectory rises at rate one: probability is compressed, not created.

When it applies

Random forcing generally adds a diffusion term. Conservation of total probability also depends on boundary flux or decay at infinity. Equal marginal densities need not mean equal path laws.

Check your understanding

Can matching one sample trajectory prove that two fields move the same distribution?

No. Distributional evolution depends on the ensemble and velocity field across the relevant region.

Chaos and the Prediction Horizon

How can a deterministic rule become difficult to predict?

The idea

Chaos does not require fresh random choices at every step. Tiny differences in initial information can grow until the requested accuracy is lost. The evolution rule, uncertainty about its starting state, and the practical prediction target are three separate issues.

A useful diagnosis combines bounded recurrent dynamics, nonperiodic structure and sensitive dependence. For Lyapunov estimation, evolve a tangent perturbation and repeatedly rescale it. Two finite-separated trajectories eventually reach the size of an attractor, so their saturated distance is not a reliable long-time growth calculation.

Read the relationship

δẋ=Df(x(t))δx; ‖δx(t)‖≈‖δx₀‖e^(λt); T≈ln(tolerance/initial error)/λ

Df is the Jacobian along the trajectory. The horizon estimate applies while approximately exponential growth is relevant and before separation saturates.

Work through an example

  1. Take λ=0.5 per second, initial error 10⁻⁶ and tolerance 10⁻².
  2. The estimated horizon is ln(10⁴)/0.5≈18.42 seconds.
  3. Tenfold better initial accuracy adds about ln(10)/0.5≈4.61 seconds, rather than multiplying the whole horizon by ten.

When it applies

Finite-time growth alone does not prove chaos: an unbounded unstable linear equation also separates exponentially. Long-term statistics may remain predictable when individual states do not.

Check your understanding

Does a bounded separation that stops growing imply a zero Lyapunov exponent?

No. Finite distances saturate; tangent growth with rescaling addresses a different quantity.

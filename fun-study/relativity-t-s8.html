Relativity / 08

Tensors, metrics, and geodesics

Why is differentiating coordinate components sometimes not enough?

The idea

A vector has both a basis and components. In curvilinear coordinates, the basis itself changes from point to point, so ordinary derivatives of components omit part of the geometric change. A connection supplies the correction needed to compare vectors at nearby points. This issue already appears in flat space written in polar coordinates.

The metric determines lengths, proper times, and inner products. In general relativity it can vary with position. Geodesics describe free-fall paths using that metric and its connection. Connection coefficients can be nonzero because of coordinate choice even when curvature is zero, so seeing Γ in an equation is not by itself evidence of gravity or curved spacetime.

Read the relationship

ds²=gμν dxμdxν; d²xμ/dτ²+Γμ_αβ(dxα/dτ)(dxβ/dτ)=0

Repeated indices are summed. g is the metric and Γ the connection coefficients; τ parametrizes a timelike geodesic by proper time here.

Work through an example

  1. In flat two-dimensional polar coordinates, a displacement uses changing radial and angular basis directions.
  2. The connection has Γʳ_φφ=−r and Γφ_rφ=Γφ_φr=1/r away from the coordinate origin.
  3. These nonzero coefficients occur despite zero intrinsic curvature, demonstrating why coordinates and geometry must be distinguished.

When it applies

A geodesic equation needs a specified metric and admissible initial data. Coordinate singularities should not be mistaken automatically for physical singularities.

Check your understanding

Does changing coordinates alter the actual length of a fixed geometric displacement?

No. Components and metric entries change together so that the computed geometric quantity stays consistent.

Orthonormal Bases and QR Factorisation

Why do right-angle coordinates make fitting problems easier?

The idea

In an orthonormal basis, each coordinate is obtained by an inner product. Gram–Schmidt constructs such directions by subtracting the projections already accounted for, then normalising what remains. This turns overlapping information into cleanly separated components.

QR records that construction as a factorisation: Q contains orthonormal directions and R records how the original columns combine them. Least squares then becomes a projection followed by an upper-triangular solve. Numerical libraries often use Householder transformations rather than classical Gram–Schmidt because finite precision can erode orthogonality.

Read the relationship

A=QR, QᵀQ=I Rx̂=Qᵀb; projection p=QQᵀb

For a tall full-column-rank m×n matrix, thin Q is m×n and R is n×n. QᵀQ=I does not imply QQᵀ=I unless Q is square.

Work through an example

  1. Let a₁=(1,0), a₂=(1,1). The first unit direction is q₁=(1,0).
  2. Subtract q₁(q₁ᵀa₂) from a₂, leaving (0,1), already unit length.
  3. Thus Q=I and R=[[1,1],[0,1]]. R retains the coefficients used to assemble the original columns.

When it applies

Dependent columns can leave a zero remainder, which cannot be normalised. Rank-revealing methods or a reduced independent set are then required.

Check your understanding

What does QQᵀ do when Q has orthonormal columns but is not square?

It projects onto the space spanned by those columns. It is not the identity on every possible output vector.

Reaction–Diffusion and Pattern Formation

How can spreading substances create patterns rather than erase them?

The idea

Reaction changes local concentrations; diffusion transfers them between nearby locations. A Turing-type mechanism starts from a uniform equilibrium stable without spatial variation. Unequal diffusion can nevertheless destabilise a band of spatial perturbations.

Linearise the reaction terms to obtain a matrix J, then examine each spatial mode. A mode with wavenumber k evolves under J−k²D, where D contains diffusivities. Positive growth identifies an early instability; nonlinear dynamics determine how a visible pattern eventually saturates.

Read the relationship

Mode growth: δu̇=(J−k²D)δu; J=[[1,−2],[2,−3]]; D=diag(0.01,1)

δu contains concentration perturbations. Here k is spatial wavenumber, not spring stiffness; the boundary determines which wavenumbers are allowed.

Work through an example

  1. Without spatial variation, J has trace −2 and determinant 1; both eigenvalues are −1.
  2. At k²=10, J−k²D=[[0.9,−2],[2,−13]], whose determinant is −7.7.
  3. A negative determinant gives one positive eigenvalue, so this spatial mode grows although the uniform reaction is stable.

When it applies

The domain must support the unstable mode. Linear growth is not a prediction of the final pattern, and not every reaction–diffusion wave is a Turing pattern.

Check your understanding

Can equal positive diffusivities destabilise a stable J by this mechanism?

Not in this constant-coefficient calculation: subtracting dk²I shifts every eigenvalue left.

Convergence, Series and Approximation Error

An infinite series converges—but how many terms are actually enough?

The idea

Three questions must be separated: does an expansion converge, what does it converge to, and how large is the error after finitely many terms? A Taylor polynomial matches derivatives locally. An infinite Taylor series requires additional justification before it can stand in for the original function.

A power series has a radius within which it converges absolutely; endpoints need their own tests. Even a convergent expansion may work slowly near its boundary. Error bounds connect the infinite statement to a practical computation by telling us what a chosen truncation leaves behind.

Read the relationship

1/(1−x)=Σₙ₌₀ᴺxⁿ+x^(N+1)/(1−x) Taylor bound: |Rn(x)|≤M|x−a|^(n+1)/(n+1)!

Rn is the truncation remainder. In the Taylor bound, M bounds the relevant derivative between the expansion point and the target point.

Work through an example

  1. Use 1+x+x²+x³ to approximate 1/(1−x).
  2. At x=0.1 the exact remainder is 0.1⁴/0.9≈0.0001111.
  3. At x=0.9 the same four terms leave remainder 0.9⁴/0.1=6.561; convergence does not make this truncation useful there.

When it applies

Smoothness does not guarantee equality with a Taylor series. Exchanging limits, sums, integrals and derivatives requires appropriate conditions, not just a plausible-looking expression.

Check your understanding

For log(1+x)'s power series, does radius 1 settle both endpoints?

No. At x=1 the alternating harmonic series converges to log2; at x=−1 the series diverges. Check endpoints individually.

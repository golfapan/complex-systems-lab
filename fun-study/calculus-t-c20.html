Series Solutions, Boundary Conditions and Eigenmodes

Why do boundaries allow only certain spatial frequencies?

The idea

For variable-coefficient equations, a power series can reveal the solution through a recurrence for its coefficients. Ordinary points and regular singular points call for different starting forms; Frobenius methods introduce a leading exponent determined by an indicial equation. Writing a formal series is only the beginning: convergence and independence still matter.

Boundary conditions can turn an ODE into an eigenvalue problem. Self-adjoint structure provides real eigenvalues and weighted orthogonality under the relevant assumptions. This makes it possible to extract coefficients by projection and express a complicated initial shape as a sum of simpler spatial modes.

Read the relationship

−(py′)′+qy=λwy Different eigenvalues ⇒ ∫ₐᵇw ym yn dx=0 −y″=λy, y(0)=y(π)=0 ⇒ λn=n²

λ is an eigenvalue, w a weight, and yn an eigenfunction. Boundary conditions are part of the operator problem, not optional decorations.

Work through an example

  1. Solve −y″=λy on 0<x<π with both endpoint values zero.
  2. Nonzero solutions occur at λn=n², with yn=sin(nx), n=1,2,….
  3. A suitable initial shape can be expanded using these modes; arbitrary frequencies do not satisfy both endpoint constraints.

When it applies

Orthogonality requires the relevant self-adjoint coefficients and boundary conditions. Repeated Frobenius roots can introduce logarithmic terms; special functions arise from specific equations and geometries.

Check your understanding

Do heat and wave equations with the same spatial eigenfunctions have identical time behaviour?

No. Heat-mode coefficients decay exponentially, while wave-mode coefficients typically oscillate. The evolution equation determines the time law.

Complex Analysis: Strong Local Rules, Powerful Integrals

Why is complex differentiability much more restrictive than ordinary differentiability in a plane?

The idea

A general real two-dimensional derivative can stretch and shear differently in different directions. Multiplication by one complex number allows only a common scaling and rotation. Requiring the same complex derivative from every direction therefore imposes the Cauchy–Riemann relations on the real and imaginary parts.

This restriction produces remarkable consequences. An analytic function is determined inside a suitable contour by its boundary values, and its derivatives can also be recovered from boundary integrals. Residues instead capture the coefficient around an isolated singularity that survives contour integration, turning local singularity information into a global integral.

Read the relationship

f=u+iv: ux=vy, uy=−vx f(z₀)=(1/(2πi))∮C f(z)/(z−z₀)dz ∮C f(z)dz=2πi Σ residues

C has positive orientation. Analyticity and singularities must be checked in the region enclosed by the contour, not only on the path.

Work through an example

  1. Integrate 1/(z−1) anticlockwise around |z|=2.
  2. The only enclosed pole is z=1, with residue 1.
  3. The residue theorem gives the entire contour integral as 2πi, without explicit path parametrisation.

When it applies

Cauchy–Riemann equations at one isolated point do not establish analyticity in a neighbourhood. Paths must avoid poles, and transform methods retain their own convergence conditions.

Check your understanding

Does every smooth real-variable function equal its Taylor series as an analytic complex function does locally?

No. Real smoothness alone is weaker than analyticity. Complex analyticity supplies much stronger local expansion guarantees.

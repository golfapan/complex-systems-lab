Gravitational clocks and the next theoretical bridges

How does a clock's location in a gravitational field affect its rate?

The idea

Clocks at different gravitational potentials can accumulate different proper times. In a static exterior Schwarzschild spacetime, a stationary clock has a rate relative to the chosen far-away time coordinate that depends on radius. A weak-field expansion connects this result to a small potential difference divided by c².

This is a comparison of specified worldlines and signal conventions, not a rule that every clock near a mass slows by the same universal amount. Motion also contributes to clock comparisons. Linking relativity with quantum theory introduces further distinctions: relativistic quantum field theory and a full quantum theory of gravity are not the same accomplishment.

Read the relationship

For a stationary exterior clock: dτ/dt=√(1−rs/r), rs=2GM/c², r>rs; weak field: Δrate≈ΔΦ/c²

The radius is the Schwarzschild radial coordinate. The stationary-clock formula assumes the stated static geometry and excludes the horizon itself.

Work through an example

  1. Near Earth's surface, use ΔΦ≈gΔh for a small height difference.
  2. Raising a clock by one metre gives an approximate fractional rate increase 9.8/(3×10⁸)²≈1.09×10⁻¹⁶.
  3. The effect is tiny but conceptually different from a clock's mechanical inaccuracy: it arises from comparing proper times in the gravitational geometry.

When it applies

Remaining stationary close to a horizon requires increasing acceleration. Do not treat the limiting formula as a physically reachable stationary clock at the horizon.

Check your understanding

Does the exterior stationary-clock formula describe every freely falling clock?

No. Free fall follows a different worldline, and its proper time must include the full motion and geometry.

Probability models, events, and counting

When is counting outcomes enough to calculate a probability?

The idea

A probability model begins with possible outcomes and a rule assigning them probabilities. An event is a collection of outcomes. Counting favorable cases and dividing by all cases works only when the elementary outcomes really are equally likely. Replacing a drawn object, changing the order of selection, or conditioning on information can change the model.

Two useful distinctions are often confused. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together. Independent events do not change each other's probability. Positive-probability events that are mutually exclusive cannot be independent: learning that one happened rules out the other.

Read the relationship

P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B); P(Aᶜ)=1−P(A)

∪ means either event, ∩ means both, and Aᶜ means not A. Independence requires P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B).

Work through an example

  1. Toss a fair coin twice, independently. The equally likely outcomes are HH, HT, TH, and TT.
  2. Three outcomes contain at least one head, giving probability 3/4.
  3. Equivalently, subtract the only failure case: 1−P(TT)=1−1/4=3/4.

When it applies

The sample space must reflect the actual experiment. Without replacement, successive draws are generally dependent.

Check your understanding

A bag contains three red and two blue balls. What is the chance of drawing two red balls without replacement?

It is (3/5)(2/4)=3/10. The second probability is conditional on the first red draw.

Likelihood and maximum likelihood estimation

Which parameter values make the observed data most plausible?

The idea

Likelihood treats the observed data as fixed and varies a model's parameter. For independent observations, the joint likelihood is a product of individual probabilities or densities. Taking logarithms turns that product into a sum without changing which parameter maximizes it, making both calculation and differentiation easier.

Maximum likelihood estimation chooses a parameter value within the assumed model. It does not by itself quantify uncertainty, justify the model, or assign probabilities to parameter values. Those are additional tasks. A sharply peaked likelihood can still be misleading when observations are dependent or the chosen model cannot represent the actual process.

Read the relationship

L(θ;D)=∏ᵢp(xᵢ|θ); ℓ(p)=k log p+(n−k)log(1−p)

D is the observed dataset; θ is the parameter. In the Bernoulli example, k successes occur in n trials.

Work through an example

  1. A coin produces seven heads in ten independent tosses. Its likelihood is proportional to p⁷(1−p)³.
  2. Differentiate: ℓ′(p)=7/p−3/(1−p). Setting this to zero gives 7−10p=0.
  3. The maximum likelihood estimate is p̂=0.7. It is a point estimate, not a claim that the true probability is known exactly.

When it applies

Likelihood is not usually a probability distribution over θ. Identifiability and boundary maxima need separate checks.

Check your understanding

If every one of four tosses is heads, what is the Bernoulli MLE?

It is p̂=1, a boundary estimate. This shows why an estimate based on little data should not be mistaken for certainty.

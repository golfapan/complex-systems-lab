Spin and the smallest quantum calculations

What can a two-component vector teach us about interference?

The idea

A qubit is a two-level quantum system represented by a normalized complex vector. Its measurement axes correspond to different observables, not to different ways of reading an ordinary hidden binary label. Small matrices let us calculate how state preparation, transformations, and measurements combine without solving a spatial wave equation.

Spin is one physical realization, but a qubit need not be imagined as a tiny classical rotating ball. The important structure is the state space and its observable operators. Operations compose through matrix multiplication, making linear algebra immediately useful for understanding quantum circuits and interference experiments.

Read the relationship

Z=[[1,0],[0,−1]]; X=[[0,1],[1,0]]; Hadamard=(1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]]

The Hadamard gate is often called H; do not confuse that gate symbol with the Hamiltonian governing time evolution.

Work through an example

  1. Start in |0⟩. One Hadamard operation gives |+⟩.
  2. A second Hadamard returns the state to |0⟩, so a zero/one measurement gives zero with certainty.
  3. Replacing the intermediate state by a classical 50/50 mixture loses coherence and fails to reproduce this prediction.

When it applies

Ideal gates are mathematical operations. Actual control pulses, noise, and readout errors require a physical implementation model.

Check your understanding

Why cannot a classical list of zero/one probabilities describe the whole intermediate state?

It omits relative phase, which the second operation converts into a measurable probability difference.

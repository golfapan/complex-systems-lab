Joint distributions, covariance, and correlation

Can two variables be related even when their correlation is zero?

The idea

A joint distribution describes variables together, including how knowing one changes the possibilities for another. Covariance measures whether centered values tend to move together linearly. Correlation removes the units by dividing by both standard deviations, making linear association easier to compare across scales.

Zero correlation does not generally imply independence. A curved relationship can have positive and negative linear contributions that cancel. Group composition can also conceal or reverse trends. Before interpreting a correlation, inspect a scatterplot, relevant subgroups, time order, and possible common causes.

Read the relationship

Cov(X,Y)=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y]; Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)+2Cov(X,Y)

Correlation is Cov(X,Y)/(σₓσᵧ) when both standard deviations are nonzero.

Work through an example

  1. Let X take −1, 0, and 1 with equal probabilities, and let Y=X².
  2. E[X]=0, E[Y]=2/3, and E[XY]=E[X³]=0, so the covariance is zero.
  3. Yet learning X=0 determines Y=0. The variables are dependent despite their zero correlation.

When it applies

Association alone does not identify a causal effect. Independence is a statement about the whole joint distribution, not just one summary.

Check your understanding

If X and Y each have variance one and covariance 0.5, what is Var(X+Y)?

It is 1+1+2×0.5=3. Ignoring dependence would underestimate the total variation.

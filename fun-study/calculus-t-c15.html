Fourier, Laplace and Convolution

How can a system's memory be expressed as a weighted sum of past inputs?

The idea

Fourier series separate a periodic signal into orthogonal harmonics. Laplace transforms organise derivatives and initial conditions algebraically. Convolution reconstructs a linear time-invariant system's output by adding the responses to many small pieces of input. They connect, but each starts with a different question.

For a causal system, an input at time τ contributes at time t according to the impulse response evaluated at its age, t−τ. Linearity allows contributions to add and time invariance allows the same response shape to be shifted. A transfer function multiplied by the input transform describes the zero-initial-condition response; stored initial state contributes separately.

Read the relationship

y(t)=∫₀ᵗh(t−τ)u(τ)dτ Laplace: L{f′}=sF(s)−f(0) Zero initial conditions: Y(s)=H(s)U(s)

h is the impulse response, u the input, and τ labels past input time. The Laplace transform requires an appropriate convergence region.

Work through an example

  1. For y′+2y=u with y(0)=0, the causal impulse response is h(t)=e⁻²ᵗ.
  2. A unit-step input gives y=∫₀ᵗe⁻²⁽ᵗ⁻τ⁾dτ.
  3. Integration yields y=(1−e⁻²ᵗ)/2, approaching the constant value 1/2.

When it applies

Fourier series need a stated convergence interpretation, especially at jumps. Transform formulas require regularity and growth conditions; do not discard initial terms.

Check your understanding

Why is the convolution upper limit t rather than infinity?

For a causal system, future inputs cannot influence the current output. Only input times up to the observation time are included.

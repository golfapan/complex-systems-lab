Observational causal inference and confounding

When can an observational comparison support an intervention claim?

The idea

Confounding occurs when treatment selection and outcomes share causes. Comparing treated and untreated groups can then combine a treatment effect with differences in who receives treatment. Adjustment first compares outcomes within suitable covariate strata, then averages using the same target-population weights.

Identification comes before estimation. Consistency, conditional exchangeability, and positivity are examples of assumptions allowing a causal quantity to be expressed through observed data. A powerful prediction model may improve estimation under these assumptions, but predictive accuracy cannot prove that no important confounder was missed. Adjusting every available variable can even introduce bias.

Read the relationship

E[Y|do(T=t)]=Σx E[Y|T=t,X=x]P(X=x); e(x)=P(T=1|X=x)

do(T=t) denotes an intervention; e(x) is a propensity score, not an outcome probability. Relevant treatment probabilities must not be zero.

Work through an example

  1. Suppose the target population is half younger and half older, and the identifying assumptions hold within age groups.
  2. Treatment-control mean differences are 8−6=2 for younger people and 14−10=4 for older people.
  3. Standardizing to the target population gives an average effect of 0.5×2+0.5×4=3.

When it applies

Post-treatment mediators and colliders require causal reasoning before adjustment. Extreme propensity weights can create unstable estimates.

Check your understanding

Can an excellent classifier predicting treatment assignment validate the no-unmeasured-confounding assumption?

No. That assumption concerns relationships with potential outcomes, including information the data may not contain.

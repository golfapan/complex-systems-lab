Regression: Projection Meets Probability

Why does a noise model sometimes lead directly to least squares?

The idea

Regression has complementary geometric, optimisation and probabilistic descriptions. Geometrically, it selects a nearby prediction; algebraically, it minimises a loss. Under independent equal-variance Gaussian errors, maximising the likelihood gives the same coefficient estimate as minimising ordinary squared residuals.

Different uncertainty assumptions change the objective. Known unequal variances assign greater influence to more reliable measurements. A Gaussian parameter prior leads to a quadratic penalty, as in ridge regression. The penalty can reduce sensitivity but introduces bias; its benefit must be assessed on data not used to tune it.

Read the relationship

y=Xβ+ε, ε~N(0,σ²I) ⇒ least-squares maximum likelihood Ridge: (XᵀX+λI)β̂=Xᵀy Generalised least squares: minβ (y−Xβ)ᵀΩ⁻¹(y−Xβ)

Ω is the known positive-definite error covariance. This ridge expression penalises all displayed coefficients; an intercept may be handled separately in practice.

Work through an example

  1. Estimate one constant from y₁=1 and y₂=3, with independent variances 1 and 4.
  2. Minimise (c−1)²+(c−3)²/4. Differentiating gives 2(c−1)+(c−3)/2=0.
  3. The weighted estimate is c=1.4, closer to the more precise first measurement than the unweighted mean 2.

When it applies

Least squares is not automatically the right likelihood for every dataset. Correct weighting depends on the error model; regularisation strength must not be selected through repeated test-set tuning.

Check your understanding

Should a measurement with larger variance receive larger or smaller precision weight?

Smaller: independent Gaussian measurements are weighted by inverse variance, reflecting lower reliability.

Partial Derivatives and the Gradient

How do several small input changes combine into one output change?

The idea

A partial derivative changes one coordinate while holding the others fixed. Differentiability is stronger: one linear approximation must explain small joint changes in every direction. For a scalar output, the coefficients of that approximation form the gradient. For a vector output, they form a Jacobian matrix.

The gradient points in the direction of fastest increase per unit distance in the chosen Euclidean coordinates. Along a regular level curve the value does not change, so the gradient is perpendicular to its tangent. Changing units or coordinate scales changes what counts as a unit move and must be considered in applications.

Read the relationship

df≈∇f·dx Dₑf=∇f·e, |e|=1 df(r(t))/dt=∇f(r(t))·r′(t)

e is a unit direction. A directional derivative measures change per unit distance; r′ includes the actual speed along a path.

Work through an example

  1. Let f(x,y)=x²+y² and travel along r(t)=(t,2t).
  2. Substitution gives f(r(t))=5t², whose derivative is 10t.
  3. The chain rule gives (2t,4t)·(1,2)=10t, agreeing with direct substitution.

When it applies

Existing partial derivatives alone do not guarantee differentiability. If variables satisfy constraints, state which quantities can change independently.

Check your understanding

At (1,1), f=x²+4y² has gradient (2,8). What is its rate along unit direction (1,1)/√2?

It is (2+8)/√2=10/√2. Along velocity (1,1), the rate per unit time is instead 10.

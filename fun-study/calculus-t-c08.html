Optimisation, Constraints and Local Shape

What changes when a problem has several directions—or a constraint?

The idea

At an interior stationary point, the gradient vanishes and second-order shape becomes important. The Hessian collects curvature and coupling between coordinates. A positive-definite Hessian makes every small nonzero direction rise; an indefinite Hessian supplies both rising and falling directions, producing a saddle.

A constraint removes some possible directions of movement. At a regular constrained optimum, the objective cannot improve to first order along allowed tangent directions. This leads to Lagrange multipliers. Least squares is another useful bridge: its best residual is perpendicular to the space of predictions the model can actually produce.

Read the relationship

H=∇²f; ∇f=0 and H positive definite ⇒ strict local minimum For g=c: ∇f=λ∇g, provided ∇g≠0 Least squares: Aᵀ(Ax̂−b)=0

λ is a multiplier, not an extra physical force by default. Positive definite means hᵀHh>0 for every h≠0.

Work through an example

  1. Maximise x+y on the unit circle x²+y²=1.
  2. The multiplier condition gives (1,1)=λ(2x,2y), hence x=y.
  3. Compare the two candidates: the maximum is √2 at (1/√2,1/√2); the opposite point gives the minimum.

When it applies

These are regular, local first- and second-order arguments. Degenerate points, boundaries and unbounded feasible sets need separate treatment; a stationary point is not automatically globally optimal.

Check your understanding

Can positive fₓₓ and fᵧᵧ alone prove a minimum?

No. Mixed terms matter: x²+4xy+y² rises along y=x but falls along y=−x.

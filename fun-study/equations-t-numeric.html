Error, Stiffness and Numerical Stability

Why can a decaying equation produce an exploding computation?

The idea

Accuracy asks how close the approximation is to the desired solution. Numerical stability asks whether the update magnifies errors or discrete modes uncontrollably. A stable result may be inaccurate, while an unstable algorithm can spoil a perfectly stable differential equation.

A stiff problem often has a smooth visible solution but fast hidden decay modes that force explicit methods to take tiny steps. The difficulty depends on the dynamics and algorithm together. Monitoring step-size refinement and independent diagnostics is more informative than judging whether a curve looks tidy.

Read the relationship

ẋ=−kx; exact step=e⁻ᵏʰ; explicit Euler R=1−kh; implicit Euler R=1/(1+kh)

R is the per-step amplification factor. For asymptotic numerical decay in this scalar test, require |R|<1.

Work through an example

  1. Set k=10 and h=0.25. The exact one-step factor is e⁻²·⁵≈0.0821.
  2. Explicit Euler gives −1.5, producing alternating growth instead of decay.
  3. Implicit Euler gives about 0.2857: stable but substantially inaccurate at this step size.

When it applies

Linear test equations reveal absolute stability regions, not every nonlinear long-time property. Roundoff, adaptive tolerances, events and conserved structures may matter too.

Check your understanding

Is agreement between two visually similar curves a complete convergence test?

No. Check quantitative errors, additional resolutions or a reference solution, and the actual quantities of interest.

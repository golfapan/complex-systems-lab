Bases and Coordinates: Changing the Description

How can the same vector have different coordinates?

The idea

A basis supplies independent directions spanning a space. Coordinates are the weights needed to assemble a vector from those directions. Choosing another basis changes those weights without moving the underlying vector. Useful coordinates often make a hidden structure visible, as in modes or principal components.

The matrix describing a linear operator must also change with the coordinate system. Convert new coordinates back to the original ones, apply the operator, then convert the result into new coordinates. This sequence produces a similarity transformation and preserves quantities such as eigenvalues and determinant, although individual matrix entries change.

Read the relationship

x=Bc, c=B⁻¹x Anew=B⁻¹AB

B's columns are the new basis vectors expressed in the original coordinates. Here B is square and invertible; c is a coordinate vector.

Work through an example

  1. Choose b₁=(1,1) and b₂=(1,−1). Find the new coordinates of x=(3,1).
  2. The coordinate equations are c₁+c₂=3 and c₁−c₂=1.
  3. They give c=(2,1), so x=2b₁+b₂. The geometric vector has not changed.

When it applies

A coordinate change and an active transformation of a physical vector are different operations. Orthogonal changes preserve Euclidean lengths; general changes of basis do not preserve coordinate lengths automatically.

Check your understanding

Why can two different matrices represent the same operator?

Their entries can refer to different bases. The operator is the action on vectors; its coordinate matrix depends on how those vectors are described.

Integrals: Adding Up Small Contributions

Why are integration and differentiation two sides of the same idea?

The idea

An integral accumulates a local quantity across a variable: velocity times a short time gives displacement; density times a short length gives mass. A definite integral is the limit of increasingly fine sums. The differential tells us what is being accumulated and helps keep the units honest.

The fundamental theorem connects accumulation to local change. If A(x) accumulates a continuous f up to x, extending the upper limit by a tiny amount adds approximately f(x) times that amount. Thus A′=f. Conversely, an antiderivative lets us replace an infinite-sum calculation with an endpoint difference.

Read the relationship

A(x)=∫ₐˣf(t)dt ⇒ A′(x)=f(x) F′=f ⇒ ∫ₐᵇf(x)dx=F(b)−F(a)

t is a dummy integration variable; x is the changing upper endpoint. A signed integral distinguishes positive and negative contributions.

Work through an example

  1. Let velocity be v(t)=2t on 0≤t≤3.
  2. Displacement is ∫₀³2t dt=[t²]₀³=9.
  3. Average velocity is 9/3=3. If velocity changed sign, total distance would instead integrate |v|.

When it applies

The familiar theorem applies under appropriate regularity, such as continuous f. An indefinite integral is a family of antiderivatives, so its arbitrary constant matters.

Check your understanding

Must you first find an elementary antiderivative to differentiate ∫₀ˣ²cos(t²)dt?

No. Apply the fundamental theorem and the chain rule to obtain 2x cos(x⁴).

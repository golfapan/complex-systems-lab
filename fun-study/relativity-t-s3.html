Proper time and moving clocks

Which time does a physical clock actually accumulate along its journey?

The idea

Proper time is the elapsed time recorded by a clock following a particular timelike worldline. Coordinate time belongs to a chosen frame; proper time belongs to the path. For uniform motion, the relationship becomes the familiar time-dilation factor, but the two events must both lie on the moving clock's worldline.

For a changing velocity, add the small proper-time contributions along the path. This path dependence is why a journey cannot be summarized by an arbitrary average velocity. It also explains why different worldlines joining suitable events can accumulate different clock readings without a contradiction about one universal time.

Read the relationship

dτ=dt√(1−u²/c²); for constant speed, Δt=γΔτ

u is the clock's speed measured in the selected inertial frame. Proper time is defined for timelike clock trajectories, not spacelike separations.

Work through an example

  1. At speed 0.6c, γ=1.25.
  2. If the lab measures ten seconds between two events on that moving clock, the clock accumulates ten divided by 1.25, or eight seconds.
  3. These are matched events on one clock; the formula is not a rule for dividing every arbitrary event time difference by γ.

When it applies

Acceleration does not invalidate proper-time integration, but an inertial-frame comparison must include the full path and its speeds.

Check your understanding

Can a massive clock follow a spacelike path and accumulate the same real proper time formula?

No. Physical massive clocks follow timelike worldlines; a spacelike interval is not a clock's elapsed time.

Real Analysis: What Makes Calculus Reliable?

When is moving a limit through an integral a legitimate step?

The idea

Real analysis studies the assumptions behind familiar calculus operations. Completeness prevents Cauchy sequences of real numbers from escaping the real number system. Compactness helps limiting candidates produce actual extrema or convergent subsequences. These are tools for existence, not merely stricter vocabulary.

Pointwise convergence treats each input separately. Uniform convergence controls every input with one common error bound, which supports stronger conclusions. On a compact interval it preserves continuity and permits integration to commute with the limit under the usual integrability conditions; differentiating a limit requires additional control.

Read the relationship

Uniform convergence: sup(x∈D)|fn(x)−f(x)|→0 |∫ₐᵇfn−∫ₐᵇf|≤(b−a)sup|fn−f|

sup is the least upper bound over the whole domain. A common bound is stronger than choosing a different convergence time at every point.

Work through an example

  1. Set fn(x)=xⁿ on [0,1]. Each fn is continuous.
  2. At every x<1 the limit is zero, while at x=1 it is one.
  3. The limiting function is discontinuous, so convergence cannot be uniform on this whole interval.

When it applies

Uniform convergence of functions alone is insufficient to exchange differentiation and limits. Different operations have different hypotheses; do not assume one convergence test licenses all of them.

Check your understanding

Why does continuity on an open bounded interval not guarantee an attained maximum?

The domain need not contain its limiting endpoints. For f(x)=x on (0,1), the supremum is 1 but no allowed input achieves it.

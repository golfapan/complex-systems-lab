Conservation Laws and Weak Solutions

How do local inflows and outflows enforce a global balance?

The idea

Begin with an accounting statement over a region: stored quantity changes through boundary transport and internal sources. Shrinking that region leads to a local conservation equation. Density and flux are different objects: density measures what is stored, while flux measures what crosses a boundary.

Nonlinear transport can turn a smooth profile into a sharp jump. Classical derivatives then fail at the jump, but an integral or weak formulation can still preserve the balance. Weak solutions may not be unique, so admissibility conditions are part of selecting a meaningful solution rather than optional decorations.

Read the relationship

uₜ+∂ₓF(u)=0; d/dt∫ₐᵇu dx=F(u(a))−F(u(b)); s=[F(uL)−F(uR)]/(uL−uR)

F is the flux, uL and uR the states beside a moving jump, and s its speed. The jump formula follows from conservation.

Work through an example

  1. For Burgers' flux F(u)=u²/2, take uL=2 and uR=0.
  2. The jump speed must be (2−0)/(2−0)=1.
  3. Swapping left and right gives the same algebraic speed, but the admissible solution is instead a spreading rarefaction: conservation alone does not select every physical solution.

When it applies

Weak formulations require appropriate test functions and initial/boundary data. Finite-volume methods express balance naturally, but their flux choices still determine accuracy and admissibility.

Check your understanding

Why is total mass constant on a periodic domain with no source?

Flux leaving one end re-enters the other; the net boundary contribution cancels.

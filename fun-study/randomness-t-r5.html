Stochastic paths and the Fokker–Planck equation

How can one process have random paths but a deterministic density equation?

The idea

A stochastic differential equation adds continuing random increments to a drift rule. Fixing the initial state no longer selects one future path. The corresponding Fokker–Planck equation describes how the distribution of those paths changes. These are two languages for different aspects of one probabilistic model.

The stochastic integral convention matters. In the Itô form, state-dependent noise contributes through a second-order density term. A deterministic density equation therefore does not remove path randomness; it summarizes it. Conversely, two processes can share each time's marginal density while having different correlations between times.

Read the relationship

dX=a(X,t)dt+b(X,t)dW; ∂tp=−∂x(ap)+½∂xx(b²p)

W is standard Brownian motion. The displayed density equation is the one-dimensional Itô form.

Work through an example

  1. For dX=−κXdt+√(2D)dW with fixed X₀, the mean is X₀e⁻κt.
  2. Its variance is (D/κ)(1−e⁻²κt), approaching D/κ when κ>0.
  3. As κ tends to zero, the variance tends to 2Dt: diffusion is recovered when the restoring drift disappears.

When it applies

Coefficients, boundary conditions, and regularity determine whether the density representation is valid. Noise conventions cannot be changed silently.

Check your understanding

If the mean tends to zero, do all sample paths eventually stop at zero?

No. The mean can vanish while persistent fluctuations maintain a nonzero stationary variance.

Initialisation and Vanishing or Exploding Gradients

Why can a signal disappear while travelling backwards through many layers?

The idea

Backpropagation multiplies Jacobians. Repeated contraction can make an early gradient tiny; repeated expansion can make it enormous. Activations, weights and depth jointly determine those products, so a single global slogan about deep networks is inadequate.

Initialisation aims to keep useful signal scales, taking activation and fan-in or fan-out into account. Gradient clipping can cap an update, but does not repair a mismatched model or loss. Inspect distributions of activations and gradients layer by layer instead of relying only on final loss.

Read the relationship

Linear sketch: g₀=(W₁ᵀ⋯Wᴸᵀ)gᴸ

The expression illustrates repeated matrix products. Real networks also include activation derivatives, residual paths and normalisation effects.

Work through an example

  1. Twenty multiplications by 0.5 leave about 9.54×10⁻⁷ of the original magnitude.
  2. Twenty multiplications by two produce 1,048,576 times that magnitude.
  3. These scalar examples explain amplification, not the exact gradient of every network.

When it applies

A bounded gradient norm does not establish informative gradients or successful learning. Saturation and dead activations may still prevent useful updates.

Check your understanding

What should you inspect when loss becomes NaN?

Check input scales, activations, gradients, overflow and the first failing operation, rather than changing many hyperparameters at once.

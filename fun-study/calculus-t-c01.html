Functions, Limits and Continuity

Can nearby inputs settle towards an answer even when the function has no value there?

The idea

A function assigns an output to each allowed input. A limit asks what those outputs approach as the input approaches a point; it does not begin by asking for the value at that point. This distinction turns an apparent hole in a formula into a question we can investigate.

Continuity joins the two stories: the limiting value must exist and agree with the actual value. On a whole closed interval, continuity also prevents jumps over intermediate values. That gives an existence argument for roots, but it neither finds their exact positions nor makes them unique.

Read the relationship

lim(x→a) f(x)=L f is continuous at a when lim(x→a)f(x)=f(a)

a is the point being approached; L is the limiting output. Inputs near a must stay inside the function's domain.

Work through an example

  1. For x≠0, let f(x)=sin(x)/x, using radians.
  2. The nearby values approach 1 as x approaches 0, although the displayed quotient is undefined at 0.
  3. Defining f(0)=1 fills the hole continuously; another choice would not.

When it applies

Continuity does not imply differentiability: |x| has a corner at zero. A root-existence argument must check continuity across the entire interval.

Check your understanding

Why does 1/x changing sign between −1 and 1 not prove a zero exists?

It is undefined at zero, so continuity on the closed interval fails. The intermediate value theorem does not apply.

Inner Products, Lengths and Orthogonality

What does it mean for two data directions to be perpendicular?

The idea

An inner product equips a vector space with geometry: lengths, angles and projections. In real Euclidean coordinates it is xᵀy. Orthogonality means this product is zero, allowing squared lengths to add by a higher-dimensional version of Pythagoras.

A norm measures size, but different norms answer different questions. Euclidean distance treats all coordinates through squares; other norms emphasise total absolute deviation or the largest component. Cosine similarity compares direction rather than magnitude. Before interpreting any distance or similarity, check whether the units and scaling of the coordinates fit the task.

Read the relationship

‖x‖₂=√(xᵀx) cosθ=xᵀy/(‖x‖₂‖y‖₂) uᵀv=0 ⇒ ‖u+v‖₂²=‖u‖₂²+‖v‖₂²

The cosine formula needs two nonzero vectors. Orthogonality always refers to a specified inner product, not merely to how a drawing looks.

Work through an example

  1. Take u=(1,1) and v=(1,−1). Their inner product is 1−1=0.
  2. Each squared length is 2.
  3. Their sum is (2,0), with squared length 4=2+2, confirming the orthogonal decomposition.

When it applies

Geometric orthogonality should not be casually equated with probabilistic independence. Uncorrelated random variables can still be dependent outside special families such as joint Gaussians.

Check your understanding

Do x and 100x have different cosine directions when x≠0?

No. Their cosine similarity is 1, even though their magnitudes differ by a factor of 100.

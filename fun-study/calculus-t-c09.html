Multiple Integrals and Coordinate Changes

Why does polar integration need that extra factor r?

The idea

Multiple integration still adds small contributions, but the pieces are now areas or volumes. Choosing coordinates can make a difficult region simple. The price is a scale factor: the same coordinate-sized rectangle may represent different physical areas at different locations.

The Jacobian describes this local stretching. Its columns are the images of small coordinate directions; the absolute determinant measures the resulting area or volume. A change of variables must transform three things together: the function, the area element, and the region with its limits. Swapping integration order likewise requires redescribing the region.

Read the relationship

dA=r dr dθ ∬D f(x,y)dxdy=∬R f(x(u,v),y(u,v))|det J|du dv

J is the derivative matrix of the coordinate map. The absolute determinant gives unsigned area; orientation is handled separately in flux integrals.

Work through an example

  1. For an ellipse x²/a²+y²/b²≤1, set x=au and y=bv, with a,b>0.
  2. The region becomes the unit disc and the Jacobian determinant is ab.
  3. Its area is therefore ab times the disc's area: πab.

When it applies

Use suitable smooth, nondegenerate coordinate maps and account for overlaps. A common sufficient condition for exchanging integration order is absolute integrability.

Check your understanding

What goes wrong if a disc's area is integrated as ∫dθdr without r?

The result scales like length rather than area. Angular changes sweep longer arcs farther from the origin, which supplies the missing factor.

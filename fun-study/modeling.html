FROM WORDS TO RELATIONSHIPS

Common modelling relationships

“Closer objects repel more strongly.” “Faster motion meets more resistance.” “Follow your neighbours.” What extra choices turn these descriptions into mathematics?

A trend does not determine a formula

“Stronger when closer” could mean 1/r, 1/r² or an exponential response. Classical electrostatic point charges have a specific inverse-square relationship; that does not make it a universal crowd-avoidance rule.

r=|xᵢ−xⱼ|; êᵢⱼ=(xᵢ−xⱼ)/r Fᵢⱼ=(kₑqᵢqⱼ/r²)êᵢⱼ = kₑqᵢqⱼ(xᵢ−xⱼ)/r³

A displacement vector already has length r. Dividing it by r³ therefore gives an inverse-square force magnitude. The lab’s Boids separation contribution uses displacement/r² before further steering normalization: it is a different construction.

Compare the choices

Closer means stronger: inverse powers

The interaction grows as separation falls.

Fᵢⱼ=(C/rᵖ) êᵢⱼ

Choose a unit vector from j towards i. Positive C then repels; negative C attracts. The words 'closer means stronger' do not determine the exponent p.

Example. Halving r multiplies an inverse-square interaction by four, but an inverse-distance interaction only by two.

Check the assumptions. Classical electrostatic point charges use a specific inverse-square law. Boids steering is not that physical law; singular behaviour at r=0 also needs a model decision.

Pull back in proportion to displacement

A larger position error produces a larger restoring response.

F=−k(x−x*)

The minus sign corrects displacement from the target x*. It does not mean 'always move left'. A pairwise spring also needs its natural separation.

Example. With k=3 and x*=0, positions +2 and −2 produce forces −6 and +6, both pointing towards the target.

Check the assumptions. Hooke's law is an elastic-range approximation. A controller can use a similar formula without a physical spring being present.

Repel nearby, attract farther away

Avoid crowding while maintaining a preferred separation.

g(r)=A/rᵖ−B/rᑫ; A,B>0; p>q>0

A short-range positive term competes with a longer-range negative term. The zero crossing supplies a preferred spacing, subject to the rest of the model.

Example. For A=B=1, p=4 and q=2, g(0.5)=12 repels, while g(2)=−0.1875 attracts.

Check the assumptions. This is one possible design, not a universal law and not the actual Boids formula. Different potentials or piecewise rules can express the same broad preference.

Define who counts as a neighbour

Only nearby agents matter, or nearby ones matter more.

Nᵢ={j:rᵢⱼ<R}; weighted mean=Σwᵢⱼaⱼ/Σwᵢⱼ

Radius, field of view, self-inclusion and distance weights define the information available to an agent. They are modelling choices, not cosmetic settings.

Example. Weights 1, 1 and 0 let two neighbours influence the mean while excluding the third.

Check the assumptions. Specify what happens with no neighbours. A hard cutoff can introduce jumps; a smooth weight changes the interaction continuously but still needs a length scale.

Sense a neighbourhood through a kernel

Summarize surrounding activity before deciding how to change.

U(x)=(K*A)(x)=∫K(x−y)A(y)dy

A kernel assigns weights across space. A ring kernel, disk kernel and local average sense different neighbourhoods. The sensed value still needs a response rule.

Example. If weights sum to one and every nearby activity equals 0.3, the sensed average is also 0.3.

Check the assumptions. Convolution alone is not growth or force. Normalization, boundaries and the response function are essential parts of Lenia-type dynamics.

Move downhill in energy or uphill in signal

Choose a direction from how a field changes around you.

F=−∇U; signal following: v=χ∇c

A gradient points uphill. A conservative force points down potential energy, whereas attraction towards a stronger signal uses the opposite sign.

Example. For U=x²/2 the force is −x. For c=x with χ>0, the signal-following velocity points right.

Check the assumptions. Not every force has a potential. Three-sensor Physarum steering approximates a preference for stronger signals without directly solving this continuous gradient equation.

Linear resistance to relative velocity

Twice the relative speed produces twice the resisting force.

Fdrag=−b(v−u); rotational damping: τ=−cω

Use motion relative to the surrounding medium, not necessarily relative to the ground. The force opposes that relative motion.

Example. If b=2 and v−u=3, the one-dimensional drag is −6. Reversing relative motion reverses drag.

Check the assumptions. This is useful in suitable low-Reynolds-number regimes or as a local approximation. Viscous angular damping is not the same as dry friction.

Resistance proportional to speed squared

Faster motion produces a more rapidly increasing resistance.

Fdrag=−(ρCᴅA/2)|vrel|vrel

The magnitude scales as relative speed squared. Multiplying |v| by the vector v retains the direction needed for a resisting force.

Example. Doubling relative speed from two to four multiplies the drag magnitude by four if the other factors remain fixed.

Check the assumptions. The coefficient and flow regime matter. The cylinder experiment solves a flow field; it is not a cylinder animated with this one drag formula.

Align with neighbouring motion

Turn towards nearby agents' average direction.

aᵢ=k(v̄Nᵢ−vᵢ); θ*=atan2(Σsinθⱼ,Σcosθⱼ)

Velocity alignment and angular alignment are related but different updates. Angles must be averaged as directions on a circle.

Example. The mean of headings +179° and −179° points near 180°, not 0°.

Check the assumptions. A zero vector sum has no unique mean direction. Boids uses capped steering; Vicsek averages headings and adds angular noise.

Move towards a local group

Do not fall behind or drift too far from neighbouring positions.

aᵢ=k(x̄Nᵢ−xᵢ)

Cohesion compares positions, whereas alignment compares motion. One agent can be on your right while flying left, so these preferences can disagree.

Example. From x=0, neighbours at two and four give a centre at three and a rightward cohesion direction.

Check the assumptions. Cohesion without separation can cause crowding. On a periodic domain, average shortest relative displacements rather than raw wrapped coordinates.

Relax towards a desired velocity

Approach a target motion gradually, or enforce a fixed speed.

a=(vdesired−v)/τ; fixed-speed projection: v←v₀v/|v|

Relaxation has a response time. Projection changes the speed immediately. They are different rules with different interpretations.

Example. A target speed of ten, current speed six and response time two give an initial acceleration of two.

Check the assumptions. Define the zero-velocity case for normalization. Fixed-speed active agents do not automatically follow the same mechanics as unconstrained massive particles.

Couple rhythms through phase differences

Adjust a rhythm according to whether neighbours lead or lag.

θ̇ᵢ=ωᵢ+(K/N)Σsin(θⱼ−θᵢ)

The sine term speeds an oscillator up or slows it down according to relative phase. Natural frequencies compete with coupling.

Example. A neighbour π/2 ahead contributes +K/N; one π/2 behind contributes −K/N.

Check the assumptions. Coupling does not guarantee complete synchronization. Network structure, frequency spread and the meaning of the order parameter all matter.

Angle-dependent torque and periodic forcing

Gravity changes with angle, or an external drive repeats in time.

θ̈=−(g/L)sinθ−γω+Acos(Ωt)

The gravitational sign shown uses the downward vertical as zero. Measuring angle from upright changes the convention.

Example. For a small angle, sinθ≈θ. At large angles that local approximation misses the bounded, periodic dependence.

Check the assumptions. A sinusoidal angle response and a sinusoidal time input have different physical sources. Damping, drive amplitude and frequency determine the resulting motion.

Lose a fixed fraction of what remains

More stored material means a larger instantaneous loss.

ċ=−λc; c(t)=c₀e⁻λᵗ; c⁺=(1−ρ)c

Exponential decay removes a proportion rather than a fixed quantity. Continuous rates and per-step fractions must be related using a time step.

Example. Losing 25% each step takes eight to six, then to 4.5, then to 3.375.

Check the assumptions. Deposition, sources and transport can balance decay. Evaporation alone does not predict the final pheromone network.

Growth limited by crowding

Growth slows as a resource or capacity limit is approached.

continuous: ẋ=rx(1−x/K); discrete: x⁺=R x(1−x)

A positive growth term competes with self-limitation. Continuous and discrete versions have different dynamical properties.

Example. The continuous growth rate vanishes at capacity K. A discrete large-parameter iteration can instead alternate or behave chaotically.

Check the assumptions. Do not infer chaos in a one-dimensional autonomous logistic ODE from the logistic map. The update rule is part of the model.

Act only when a condition is met

Enough neighbours cause birth, or enough stored grains cause toppling.

condition → action; (state,input) → (new state,output)

A model can be a logical rule rather than a force equation. Thresholds, lookup tables and finite-state machines describe different kinds of discrete decisions.

Example. A dead Life cell with three live neighbours is born; one with two is not. A sandpile site with four grains can topple.

Check the assumptions. Synchronous versus asynchronous updates and edge handling can change behaviour. A smooth derivative need not exist across a threshold.

Multiply variables to express interaction

The response depends on how much of several ingredients is present.

u̇=−uv²+F(1−u); v̇=uv²−(F+k)v

A product term represents nonlinear coupling; its sign states which field is consumed and which is produced in this model.

Example. Holding v fixed and doubling u doubles uv². Holding u fixed and doubling v multiplies it by four.

Check the assumptions. These are Gray–Scott-type terms, not a universal chemistry law. Activator–inhibitor and excitable systems can use quite different response functions.

Spread from high values towards low values

A local excess tends to flatten into its surroundings.

uₜ=DΔu; U̇ᵢ=D(Uᵢ₊₁−2Uᵢ+Uᵢ₋₁)/h²

The Laplacian compares a location with its neighbourhood. It is different from carrying a shape with a velocity field.

Example. A centre value one between two zero neighbours has a negative second difference; zero between two ones has a positive one.

Check the assumptions. Mass conservation depends on boundary fluxes. Explicit numerical updates also need a suitable step size; diffusion alone tends to smooth rather than create persistent patterns.

Carry a field with a flow

An existing velocity transports dye, heat or another quantity.

cₜ+u·∇c=0; conserved density: cₜ+∇·(cu)=0

Advection moves a pattern. A compressible flow also changes density through compression, so the two displayed forms are not always interchangeable.

Example. At constant one-dimensional velocity two, c(x,t)=c₀(x−2t): after one second the shape has moved right by two units.

Check the assumptions. Interpolation can introduce numerical spreading even when the ideal transport equation does not. Distinguish this from physical diffusion.

Account for inflow, outflow and sources

A total changes because something crosses its boundary or is created or removed.

ρₜ+∇·J=s; incompressibility: ∇·u=0

Conservation is a statement about a specified quantity and boundary. Buoyancy supplies momentum while thermal diffusion redistributes temperature.

Example. An internal sandpile toppling sends four grains to four neighbours without loss. At an open edge, some grains leave the domain.

Check the assumptions. State what is conserved and which sources are present. A buoyancy approximation also needs a temperature scale, gravity direction and validity conditions.

Specify a distribution of random increments

The next step is uncertain, but its statistical rule is known.

X⁺=X+ℓξ, ξ=±1 equally likely; dX=b dt+σdW

Ongoing random driving differs from merely randomizing the initial state. Independence and the increment distribution are part of the specification.

Example. After n independent symmetric steps, mean displacement is zero while variance is nℓ².

Check the assumptions. Zero mean does not mean every walker returns home. DLA adds a sticking rule, while Vicsek noise acts on angle rather than directly on position.

Turn preferences into probabilities

A better option is more likely, without necessarily being chosen every time.

P(j)=wⱼ/Σwₖ; Metropolis: Paccept=min(1,e^(−ΔE/T))

Normalize nonnegative weights over available options. Energy-biased acceptance also depends on the proposal mechanism.

Example. Weights one and three give probabilities 1/4 and 3/4. With ΔE=2 and T=1, an uphill Metropolis proposal is accepted with probability e⁻².

Check the assumptions. The displayed Metropolis form assumes symmetric proposals. A generic noise parameter is not automatically a physical temperature. The exponential form assumes T>0; at T=0 accept non-increasing-energy proposals and reject uphill ones.

Leave a trace that influences later choices

Past actions alter the environment and bias future actions.

τ⁺=(1−ρ)τ+deposits; P(i→j)∝τᵢⱼᵅ/dᵢⱼᵝ

Reinforcement and forgetting compete. Distance preferences are weights before they become normalized probabilities.

Example. With the lab's weight τ/d³, halving distance multiplies a candidate weight by eight if pheromone stays fixed.

Check the assumptions. Langton's ant has no pheromone mechanism. Physarum sensing and ant-colony tour construction are different feedback designs.

Correct an error using feedback

Choose an action from the current deviation and its rate of change.

u=−Kp e−Kd ė; state feedback: u=−K(x−x*)

A proportional term restores position; a derivative term responds to motion. Actuator saturation can prevent the requested action.

Example. For e=0.1, ė=0.2, Kp=30 and Kd=6, the request is −4.2; a magnitude limit of three clips it to −3.

Check the assumptions. Negative feedback can still become unstable through inappropriate gains, delay or saturation. Local LQR balance is not a global swing-up guarantee.

Learn a shared local update

Use data to choose the rule, then apply that rule repeatedly.

hᵢ⁺=hᵢ+fθ(local perception); minimize a rollout loss

Every cell can share the same parameters while producing different updates because its local state differs. Evolution and parameter training are separate processes.

Example. A small NCA is trained to resemble a disk after twenty steps. Matching that final picture is a specific, testable task.

Check the assumptions. A successful fixed-horizon image does not establish persistence, arbitrary scaling or regeneration after damage. Test each claimed behaviour separately.

Couple variables and repeat nonlinear rules

The effect of one quantity depends on another, or an update contains powers.

ż=xy−βz; complex iteration: z⁺=z²+c

Products and powers violate simple superposition. They can create rich dynamics, but nonlinearity alone does not imply chaos.

Example. Doubling x and y multiplies xy by four. With c=0, repeated squaring takes z₀=0.5 towards zero and z₀=2 rapidly away.

Check the assumptions. Lorenz is a continuous coupled system; Mandelbrot and Julia pictures use complex discrete iteration. Shared mathematical ingredients do not make them equivalent models.

One model usually combines several relationships

Boids mixes separation, alignment, cohesion and a speed constraint. Reaction–diffusion combines local response with spatial spreading. Ant-colony search combines weighted choices, deposits and forgetting. Cellular automata may instead use direct logical updates.

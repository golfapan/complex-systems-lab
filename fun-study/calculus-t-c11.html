Green, Gauss and Stokes: Inside Meets Boundary

How does adding local behaviour across a region leave only its boundary?

The idea

These integral theorems express a shared cancellation principle. Divide a region into small pieces and add their boundary contributions: shared internal boundaries appear with opposite orientations and cancel. What remains is the outside boundary, linking local derivatives to an overall measurement.

The derivatives measure different things. Divergence is local net outflow per volume; curl measures local circulation per area. The Laplacian is the divergence of a gradient, not the square of a gradient. Choosing the right theorem means matching the derivative, integration region and boundary measurement.

Read the relationship

Gauss: ∯∂Ω F·n dS=∭Ω div F dV Stokes: ∮∂S F·dr=∬S curl F·n dS Green: ∮∂D Pdx+Qdy=∬D(Qₓ−Pᵧ)dA

n is the chosen unit normal. Gauss uses the outward normal; Stokes pairs the normal and boundary direction by the right-hand rule.

Work through an example

  1. Take F=(x,y,z), so div F=3.
  2. On a radius-R sphere, F·n=R; flux is R(4πR²)=4πR³.
  3. The volume integral gives 3(4πR³/3)=4πR³, the same result.

When it applies

The standard forms need appropriate smoothness and oriented boundaries or surfaces. Holes and singularities may require extra boundaries or a different formulation.

Check your understanding

Does a large velocity at one point necessarily imply large divergence?

No. Divergence concerns spatial variation and net local outflow, not simply the velocity's magnitude.

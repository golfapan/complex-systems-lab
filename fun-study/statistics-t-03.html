Conditional probability and Bayesian updating

Why can an accurate alarm still produce many false alarms?

The idea

Conditional probability changes the set of possibilities under consideration. P(A|B) asks about A among cases where B occurred. Bayes' rule reverses the direction of conditioning by combining a likelihood with a prior probability. It does not allow us to simply swap the labels on a probability.

Base rates matter because evidence may have several sources. An alarm can come from a real fault or from a healthy machine. Bayesian updating compares both routes. When combining evidence, count genuinely new information: two reports copied from the same source are not two independent observations.

Read the relationship

P(H|E)=P(E|H)P(H)/P(E); P(E)=ΣⱼP(E|Hⱼ)P(Hⱼ)

H is a hypothesis, E is evidence, and the Hⱼ form mutually exclusive, exhaustive alternatives.

Work through an example

  1. Suppose 1% of machines are faulty, an alarm detects 90% of faults, and 5% of healthy machines raise an alarm.
  2. Among 10,000 machines, expect 90 true alarms and 495 false alarms.
  3. The fault probability after an alarm is 90/(90+495)≈15.38%, despite the high detection rate.

When it applies

Likelihood is not posterior probability. Multiplying likelihoods requires the appropriate conditional independence assumptions.

Check your understanding

If the fault rate rises to 10%, with other rates unchanged, what is the alarm's posterior fault probability?

It becomes 0.09/(0.09+0.045)=2/3. The same alarm has a different meaning in a different population.

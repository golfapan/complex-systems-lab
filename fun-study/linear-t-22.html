Markov Chains, Graphs and State Propagation

What changes when a state vector contains probabilities rather than positions?

The idea

A transition matrix advances a probability distribution through possible states. Here each row contains the next-state probabilities given a current state, so distributions are row vectors. Nonnegative entries and row sums of one preserve total probability, but do not alone guarantee convergence to one common limiting distribution.

Stationary distributions are left eigenvectors with eigenvalue one. For a finite irreducible aperiodic chain, the stationary distribution is unique and every initial distribution converges to it. Related graph matrices describe a different object: the Laplacian measures how much neighbouring node values disagree and supports smoothing and spectral clustering.

Read the relationship

pt+1=ptP; πP=π; Σπi=1 For an undirected nonnegative weighted graph: xᵀLx=(1/2)Σijwij(xi−xj)²≥0

P is row-stochastic in this convention. L=D−W, with symmetric nonnegative adjacency weights W and weighted-degree diagonal D.

Work through an example

  1. Take P=[[0.9,0.1],[0.2,0.8]] and write π=(a,1−a).
  2. The first stationary equation is a=0.9a+0.2(1−a), giving 0.3a=0.2.
  3. Therefore π=(2/3,1/3). The other eigenvalue is 0.7, so initial discrepancies decay in this example.

When it applies

Some texts use column-stochastic matrices, transposing the formulas. Reducibility or periodicity invalidates the simple convergence claim; a Markov chain becomes a decision model only after adding actions, rewards and policies.

Check your understanding

Does existence of a stationary distribution prove convergence to it from every start?

No. For example, a deterministic two-state alternation is periodic: it has a stationary distribution but individual starting distributions keep alternating.

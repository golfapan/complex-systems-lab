Flow Maps and Semigroups

How does an instantaneous rule become a finite-time prediction?

The idea

A vector field tells you where a state is heading now. A flow map tells you where that initial state arrives after a specified duration. Computing the flow is generally the original differential-equation problem, packaged as an initial-to-final-state map.

For an autonomous system with unique solutions, evolving for s and then t agrees with evolving for s+t, wherever both operations exist. This composition rule explains semigroup language. Calling the maps a group additionally requires suitable backward evolution and inverses within the state space being considered.

Read the relationship

ẋ=−2x; Φᵗ(x₀)=e^(−2t)x₀; Φᵗ⁺ˢ=Φᵗ∘Φˢ

Φᵗ is a map indexed by elapsed time, not a power of a number. The symbol ∘ means composition.

Work through an example

  1. Begin with x₀=3 and evolve for 0.5 seconds: the result is 3e⁻¹.
  2. Another second gives 3e⁻³≈0.14936, exactly as one 1.5-second evolution does.
  3. An Euler step with h=0.5 instead gives (1−2h)x=0, showing that a numerical step is a different map.

When it applies

Explicitly time-dependent systems generally require a two-time evolution map Φ(t,s). Blow-up, nonuniqueness and irreversible evolution limit which compositions or inverses exist.

Check your understanding

Does knowing the one-second sampling map uniquely identify the continuous rule?

Not generally. Sampling loses information; reconstructing a continuous generator needs additional assumptions.

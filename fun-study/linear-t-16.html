Hessians, Curvature and Optimisation Steps

Why can one learning rate work well in one direction and fail in another?

The idea

The gradient describes first-order change, while the Hessian describes how that change bends and couples across directions. For a sufficiently smooth scalar function the Hessian is symmetric. At a stationary point, its quadratic form distinguishes a strict local bowl, a peak and a saddle when the corresponding definiteness tests are decisive.

Optimisation uses this geometry in different ways. Gradient descent takes one scaled negative-gradient step. Newton's method solves a linear system that adjusts for local curvature. Strongly unequal positive curvature creates directions with very different natural step sizes, linking optimisation speed to spectral conditioning.

Read the relationship

f(x+δ)≈f(x)+∇f(x)ᵀδ+δᵀHδ/2 Newton: Hδ=−∇f Gradient descent: xnew=x−η∇f

H is the Hessian, η the learning rate and δ a trial step. The quadratic approximation is local, not an exact global representation in general.

Work through an example

  1. For f(x,y)=(x²+100y²)/2, H=diag(1,100).
  2. With η=0.01, the update is xnew=0.99x and ynew=0.
  3. With η=0.03, the y multiplier becomes 1−3=−2, causing divergence. This quadratic needs 0<η<0.02 for convergence.

When it applies

A semidefinite Hessian can leave classification unresolved. For a general nonconvex objective, a Newton step need not descend without safeguards.

Check your understanding

Does a small gradient imply the point is a good minimum?

Not necessarily. Saddles and maxima can also have zero gradient; inspect curvature, surrounding behaviour and the task's feasible region.

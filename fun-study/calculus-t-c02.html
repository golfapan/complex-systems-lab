Derivatives as Local Models

What does a derivative predict, beyond the slope of a tangent?

The idea

A derivative is the local conversion factor between a small input change and the resulting output change. The tangent line is useful because its error becomes small relative to the input step as that step shrinks. It is a local model, not a claim that the original relationship is globally straight.

The chain rule passes these small changes through successive stages: multiply the gain of the outer stage by that of the inner stage. A second derivative adds curvature, helping explain which way a tangent estimate is likely to miss. Reliable error bounds require information over a neighbourhood, not just at one point.

Read the relationship

f(a+h)=f(a)+f′(a)h+o(h) (f∘g)′(x)=f′(g(x))g′(x)

h is a small input step. The remainder o(h), divided by h, tends to zero; it is not generally exactly zero.

Work through an example

  1. For f(x)=√x, choose the convenient point a=4: f(4)=2 and f′(4)=1/4.
  2. The input change from 4 to 4.04 is h=0.04.
  3. The linear estimate is √4.04≈2+(1/4)(0.04)=2.01.

When it applies

The differential dy=f′(x)dx is a linear prediction; the actual change Δy can differ. Far from the expansion point, omitted terms can matter.

Check your understanding

If a sphere's radius has a small 1% error, what volume error does a derivative predict?

Since V is proportional to r³, dV/V=3dr/r: approximately 3%, for sufficiently small relative errors.

Fokker–Planck Equations and Probability Flow

How can random paths have a deterministic density equation?

The idea

A Fokker–Planck equation predicts the probability density of a stochastic process. Drift transports probability; diffusion spreads it. A deterministic equation for the density does not make an individual realisation deterministic: the prediction object has changed.

For spatially constant scalar noise, the diffusion term has a Laplacian form. More general state-dependent diffusion needs the full second-order expression. Under suitable conditions, rewriting this density evolution as transport gives a probability-flow ODE with the same time marginals but different paths.

Read the relationship

dX=fdt+g(t)dW; ∂ₜp=−∇·(fp)+(g²/2)Δp; v=f−(g²/2)∇log p

The score is ∇log p. Matching time marginals means matching each single-time distribution, not every cross-time joint distribution.

Work through an example

  1. For dX=−aXdt+σdW with a>0 and fixed initial value x₀, the mean is x₀e⁻ᵃᵗ.
  2. The variance is σ²(1−e⁻²ᵃᵗ)/(2a). For a=1 and σ=2 it approaches two.
  3. This Gaussian density is a useful simulation check; demanding that independent paths coincide would test the wrong object.

When it applies

Boundary flux, singular initial distributions and density regularity matter. A probability-flow trajectory is not a Brownian sample path merely because their marginals agree.

Check your understanding

Do all single-time histograms determine temporal correlations?

No. Marginal distributions do not specify how values at different times are coupled.

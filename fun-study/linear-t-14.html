Conditioning and Reliable Computation

Can a unique exact solution still be practically unreliable?

The idea

Conditioning measures sensitivity inherent in a problem: how much a small input perturbation can change the desired answer. Algorithmic stability asks whether the computation adds avoidable error. A well-implemented solver cannot remove uncertainty already magnified by an ill-conditioned model.

Singular values identify the sensitive directions. A small minimum singular value means some large input change produces only a tiny output change, so reversing the map magnifies noise. Scaling, QR, SVD and regularisation address different parts of the difficulty. In particular, forming normal equations squares the spectral condition number for a full-column-rank matrix.

Read the relationship

For invertible A: κ₂(A)=σmax/σmin ‖δx‖/‖x‖≤κ₂(A)‖δb‖/‖b‖, with fixed A

This relative right-hand-side perturbation bound assumes nonzero b and x. A singular matrix has infinite condition number in this sense.

Work through an example

  1. Use A=diag(1,0.001), b=(1,0). The exact solution is (1,0).
  2. Change b to (1,0.001), a relative perturbation of 0.001.
  3. The new solution is (1,1), a relative change of 1: a factor-1000 amplification, matching κ₂(A)=1000.

When it applies

A small residual does not guarantee a small solution error. More displayed decimal places do not recover information absent from the measurements.

Check your understanding

Can numerical stability compensate fully for an ill-conditioned inverse problem?

No. It avoids adding unnecessary error, but the problem's sensitivity remains. Better data, reformulation or explicit regularisation may be needed.

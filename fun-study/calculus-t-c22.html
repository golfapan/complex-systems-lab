Modelling, Scales and Conservation

Before solving an equation, how do we know it describes the intended problem?

The idea

Modelling begins by defining state variables, parameters, units, assumptions and the quantity being predicted. Conservation accounts for storage and transport, but does not usually close a model on its own. A constitutive relation explains how flux or force depends on state, supplying the mechanism needed for an equation.

Nondimensionalisation separates meaningful parameter combinations from arbitrary units. Choose scales, substitute them into every term, then simplify. Similar dimensionless groups can explain why systems of different physical sizes behave alike. Refining a mesh checks numerical error; it does not automatically repair a missing physical mechanism or an incorrectly estimated parameter.

Read the relationship

ut+Jx=0, J=−Dux ⇒ ut=Duxx x=Lx*, t=Tt* ⇒ coefficient DT/L² Diffusion time scale: T=L²/D

J is flux, D>0 a diffusivity, and stars mark dimensionless variables. A sign error in the flux law reverses the predicted mechanism.

Work through an example

  1. Diffusion across length L has characteristic time L²/D.
  2. Keep D fixed and double the size to 2L.
  3. The characteristic time becomes (2L)²/D=4L²/D: four times as long, not twice.

When it applies

A good fit is not proof of the mechanism. Check units, initial and boundary data, limiting cases and independent predictions before trusting a model outside its calibration range.

Check your understanding

What distinguishes conservation from a constitutive law?

Conservation tracks the balance of a quantity. A constitutive law specifies how the quantity moves or responds, such as flux down a concentration gradient.

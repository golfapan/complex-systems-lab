Quantum Physics / 01

From measurement records to quantum states

What does a wavefunction let you predict?

The idea

A quantum state is a mathematical object used to predict measurement probabilities. In a position representation, a wavefunction ψ(x) supplies a complex amplitude for each position. The Born rule converts its squared magnitude into a probability density. A detector's finite interval therefore receives probability by integration, not by reading the amplitude itself as a probability.

Normalization makes the total probability one. It constrains the whole state rather than forcing every amplitude to lie between zero and one. Different representations can describe the same state, just as different coordinate systems describe the same vector. The choice of measurement determines which representation is convenient.

Read the relationship

∫|ψ(x)|²dx=1; P(a≤X≤b)=∫ₐᵇ|ψ(x)|²dx

ψ is an amplitude, |ψ|² a position density, and X the position outcome. Amplitude, density, and integrated probability have different meanings and units.

Work through an example

  1. Prepare a normalized wavefunction equal to 1/√L inside an interval of length L and zero outside.
  2. Its position density inside is 1/L.
  3. The first quarter of the interval contains probability (L/4)(1/L)=1/4; this is not automatically an energy eigenstate of a box.

When it applies

A normalized function can still have additional physical-domain restrictions for a particular Hamiltonian. Preparation and time evolution require the full model.

Check your understanding

Can the wavefunction itself be negative or complex?

Yes. Probabilities come from amplitudes through the Born rule; amplitudes also carry phase information.

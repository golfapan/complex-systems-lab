Differentiability and Local Inverse Maps

Why are derivatives along coordinate axes not enough in several variables?

The idea

Differentiability means a single linear map predicts the change along every sufficiently small approach, with an error negligible relative to distance. Partial derivatives inspect only coordinate directions. Even having directional derivatives does not by itself provide the uniform control required by a full derivative.

A nonsingular Jacobian means the local linear model has not flattened any direction. With suitable regularity, the inverse function theorem turns that into local invertibility. The implicit function theorem similarly lets a regular constraint be solved locally for selected variables. Neither theorem promises a global inverse or a single branch everywhere.

Read the relationship

f(a+h)=f(a)+Df(a)h+o(‖h‖) f∈C¹ and det Df(a)≠0 ⇒ local inverse F(x,y)=0, Fy≠0 ⇒ y′=−Fx/Fy

Df is the full derivative; its coordinate matrix is the Jacobian. C¹ means continuous first partial derivatives in a neighbourhood.

Work through an example

  1. For the unit circle, F(x,y)=x²+y²−1.
  2. Near (0,1), Fy=2y≠0, so y=√(1−x²) is a valid smooth local description.
  3. At (1,0), Fy=0 and this y-as-a-function-of-x description fails; the circle itself is not singular.

When it applies

Coordinate singularities need not be geometric singularities. Multiple constraints require the appropriate rank condition, not merely a list of formal multiplier equations.

Check your understanding

Does a locally invertible polar-coordinate map provide one unrestricted global angle?

No. Angles differing by 2π represent the same point. A global single-valued inverse needs a suitable restriction or several coordinate patches.

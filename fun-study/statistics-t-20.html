Convexity, constraints, and Newton's method

When can optimization provide more than a plausible answer?

The idea

Convexity supplies a useful global structure: a local minimum of a convex objective over a convex feasible set is also global. Constraints state which answers are allowed. At a boundary, the best feasible solution may have a nonzero unconstrained gradient, because the improving direction points outside the permitted region.

Newton's method uses curvature to choose a direction by solving a linear system involving the Hessian. It can be very effective near a well-behaved solution, but it needs safeguards outside that setting. Lagrange multipliers and KKT conditions express how objective improvement balances active constraints under appropriate regularity assumptions.

Read the relationship

H(θ)Δ=−∇J(θ); ℒ(θ,λ)=J(θ)+Σλⱼgⱼ(θ), with gⱼ≤0 and λⱼ≥0

H is the Hessian. KKT includes feasibility, stationarity, and complementary slackness; it is not just one derivative equation.

Work through an example

  1. Minimize (w−3)² subject to w≤1. The unconstrained optimum is infeasible.
  2. The closest feasible point is w*=1, where the objective is four.
  3. For ℒ=(w−3)²+λ(w−1), stationarity gives −4+λ=0, so λ=4, and complementary slackness holds.

When it applies

Nonconvex problems can have multiple local solutions. Newton steps may need damping or line search; constraint qualifications matter for multiplier claims.

Check your understanding

Why is a nonzero derivative at w=1 compatible with constrained optimality?

Because moving toward the unconstrained minimum would violate w≤1. Feasible directions, rather than arbitrary directions, determine improvement.

Gradient Flows, Dissipation and Optimisation

Why does moving down a gradient decrease an objective?

The idea

A gradient flow uses the negative gradient as a continuous velocity. In Euclidean coordinates this is the steepest local descent direction. The evolving state can be a model parameter, but that does not make optimisation identical to every physical dissipative process.

The continuous equation has a useful loss-decrease identity. A computer, however, takes finite updates: a large step can overshoot the minimum, oscillate or diverge. Changing the metric, adding momentum or using sampled gradients changes the dynamics and the assumptions behind that identity.

Read the relationship

θ̇=−∇L(θ); dL/dt=−‖∇L‖²; θₙ₊₁=θₙ−η∇L(θₙ)

θ is the parameter vector, η the discrete learning rate and ∇L the gradient. Continuous time and update number are different variables.

Work through an example

  1. Take L(θ)=θ²/2. The continuous solution is θ(t)=θ₀e⁻ᵗ.
  2. Gradient descent becomes θₙ₊₁=(1−η)θₙ. At η=1.5, errors alternate and halve.
  3. At η=2.2, each error is multiplied by −1.2 and grows, despite the decreasing continuous model.

When it applies

Loss decrease does not guarantee a unique global minimum. SGD and Adam introduce noise or additional internal state and cannot inherit every fixed gradient-flow conclusion.

Check your understanding

Does L̇≤0 imply constant-speed convergence to one optimum?

No. Speed varies with the gradient, and uniqueness, convergence and rates require additional assumptions.

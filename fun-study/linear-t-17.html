Covariance and the Geometry of Random Vectors

How does a matrix describe the shape of uncertainty?

The idea

A random vector's mean locates its centre, while its covariance describes how components vary together. Variances occupy the diagonal; cross-covariances record joint variation. The covariance matrix is symmetric positive semidefinite because every directional variance must be nonnegative.

Linear transformations carry means and covariances through exact matrix formulas whenever second moments exist. For a nonsingular Gaussian distribution, these quantities determine the full distribution, with covariance eigenvectors and eigenvalues describing elliptical shape. For general distributions, matching means and covariances does not identify all dependence or the whole probability law.

Read the relationship

μ=E[X]; Σ=E[(X−μ)(X−μ)ᵀ] Y=AX+b ⇒ E[Y]=Aμ+b, Cov(Y)=AΣAᵀ

Σ is covariance, not a data matrix. A singular Σ allows uncertainty restricted to a lower-dimensional subspace.

Work through an example

  1. Let X₁ and X₂ be independent, each with mean zero and variance one.
  2. Set Y₁=2X₁ and Y₂=X₁+X₂, so A=[[2,0],[1,1]].
  3. Cov(Y)=AAᵀ=[[4,2],[2,2]]. The outputs are correlated because both contain X₁.

When it applies

Zero covariance does not generally imply independence; the joint Gaussian case is special. Nonlinear transformations usually need more than a Jacobian approximation for exact uncertainty propagation.

Check your understanding

Can you use an ordinary full-dimensional Gaussian density formula with singular covariance?

Not directly. Its mass is confined to a subspace, so the usual inverse and determinant density formula does not apply unchanged.

Observables, eigenvalues, and measurement

Why can an average measurement value be an outcome you never observe?

The idea

For a finite-dimensional or discrete-spectrum ideal projective measurement, a self-adjoint observable has eigenvalues as possible recorded results, with projectors describing the corresponding outcome subspaces. The Born rule determines their probabilities from the prepared state. An expectation value is an ensemble average over repeated preparations, not an additional possible result. Continuous-spectrum observables such as position require a spectral-measure description rather than this discrete sum.

An eigenstate of the measured observable can give a certain result. A superposition of its eigenspaces generally gives a distribution. This distinction already prevents the misleading claim that every quantum measurement must be uncertain. The same state can be definite for one observable and uncertain for another.

Read the relationship

A=Σa aΠa; P(a)=⟨ψ|Πa|ψ⟩; ⟨A⟩=⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩

Πa projects onto the outcome-a subspace in the discrete measurement shown. In finite dimensions, self-adjoint means a Hermitian matrix; infinite-dimensional operators require domain and spectral-measure care.

Work through an example

  1. Let Z=diag(1,−1) and prepare |+⟩.
  2. Measurements return +1 and −1 with equal probabilities, giving expectation zero.
  3. No single Z measurement returns zero. Preparing |0⟩ instead makes the result +1 certain.

When it applies

Real measurement procedures can be more general than ideal projective measurements. Always specify the observable or operational measurement model.

Check your understanding

If an average is zero, must the state be an eigenstate with eigenvalue zero?

No. Positive and negative outcomes can average to zero even when zero is not an allowed outcome.

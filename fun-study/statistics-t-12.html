Probability and Statistics / 12

Hypothesis tests, effect sizes, and multiple comparisons

How surprising would these data be if a particular null model were true?

The idea

A hypothesis test starts with a null model and a statistic sensitive to a relevant departure. The p-value is the probability, under that null model, of a result at least as extreme by the chosen criterion. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the probability that the finding will replicate.

Statistical significance should be accompanied by an effect estimate, an uncertainty interval, and an explanation of practical importance. Testing many possibilities creates extra opportunities for a small p-value. A multiple-comparison method or a prespecified analysis helps make the strength of evidence easier to interpret.

Read the relationship

z=(x̄−μ₀)/(σ/√n); Bonferroni per-test threshold=αfamily/m

μ₀ is the null mean, σ is a known population standard deviation, and m is the number of tests in the family.

Work through an example

  1. Test μ₀=100 with n=25, sample mean 104, and known σ=10.
  2. The standard error is two, so z=2 and the two-sided normal p-value is about 0.0455.
  3. The estimated difference is four; its 95% interval is about [0.08,7.92]. Whether that matters depends on the application.

When it applies

Failure to reject does not prove no effect. Test assumptions, optional stopping, model selection, and dependent comparisons can change the interpretation.

Check your understanding

If p=0.03, can you say the null has a 3% chance of being true?

No. The probability is calculated conditional on the null model, not as a posterior probability of that model.

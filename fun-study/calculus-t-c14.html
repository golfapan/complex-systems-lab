Forcing, Particular Solutions and Resonance

Which part of a response comes from the system, and which from the input?

The idea

A forced linear equation combines a homogeneous response with one particular solution. Subtracting any two solutions to the same forcing cancels the input and leaves the homogeneous equation. If all natural modes decay, initial-condition effects fade and a persistent input can dominate the long-term behaviour.

A sinusoidal input can be organised with complex exponentials: the transfer factor changes amplitude and phase, then taking the real part recovers the physical signal. Resonance is a special interaction between input and natural modes. Damping changes both peak height and, often, the frequency at which the peak occurs.

Read the relationship

L[y]=f ⇒ y=yh+yp H(iω)=1/(ω₀²−ω²+2iζω₀ω) y″+y=cos t has yp=(t/2)sin t

yh solves the zero-input equation; yp is one solution for the specified input. ζ is the damping ratio and ω₀ the undamped natural frequency.

Work through an example

  1. For y″+y=cos t, guessing A cos t fails because cos t is already a homogeneous mode.
  2. Try yp=At sin t; differentiation gives yp″+yp=2A cos t.
  3. Choose A=1/2. The ideal undamped resonant response has a linearly growing envelope.

When it applies

Unbounded resonant growth is an ideal undamped result, not the behaviour of every driven oscillator. At fixed forcing, adding two complete solutions doubles the forcing.

Check your understanding

If y1 and y2 satisfy L[y]=f, what equation does y1−y2 satisfy?

The homogeneous equation L[y1−y2]=0. This explains why all solutions share the same particular-solution part up to a homogeneous addition.

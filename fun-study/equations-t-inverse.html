Inverse Problems and Well-Posedness

Can data reveal the unknown conditions that produced them?

The idea

A forward problem predicts observations from specified parameters and conditions. An inverse problem recovers some of those unknowns from observations. Difficulties may involve no solution, multiple explanations or extreme sensitivity to small data errors.

Regularisation adds a preference for smoother, smaller or otherwise plausible solutions. This can improve stability but introduces assumptions and bias. A low objective value cannot establish that the recovered explanation is uniquely determined by the data; experimental design and identifiability come first.

Read the relationship

y=H(uθ)+ε; minimise ‖H(uθ)−y‖²+λR(θ); u(t)=u₀e⁻ᵏᵗ

H is the observation operator, θ the unknown, ε observation noise and R a regularisation penalty. λ controls the trade-off.

Work through an example

  1. If u₀=1 and a noise-free observation at t=1 is e⁻², then k=−ln(e⁻²)=2.
  2. If u₀ is also unknown, the same observation is explained by u₀=e⁻¹ and k=1.
  3. One reading therefore cannot identify both parameters. More optimiser iterations do not supply the missing independent information.

When it applies

Neural-network flexibility and regularisation do not remove structural nonidentifiability. Assess stability under noise and test predictions on independent data.

Check your understanding

Does almost-zero training error prove the inverse solution is correct?

No. Different parameters may fit equally well; uniqueness and stability need separate evidence.
